Lesson 03 · Content

Purpose and Structure

The two content instruments: what the speech was for, and whether the audience could follow it there.

Mission: judge purpose and structure from the audience's seat Time: ~20 min reading + ~30 min drills Prerequisite: Lesson 02

Lesson 02 gave you the map. This lesson goes into the half of it that has no moment — the half you cannot watch happening. Purpose and structure are properties of a whole speech: neither occurs at any particular second, so neither can be observed. They can only be reconstructed. That is why they are the findings most evaluators never report, and the ones that change what a speaker does next.

Section 1Purpose: the contract, then the standard

Start with the sentence that separates purpose from everything else you evaluate:

Every speech has a general purpose whether it is defined by the speaker or not.

Toastmasters International, Writing a Speech With Purpose (Item 8103), p. 131

That is not a definition; it is a warning. The general purpose — the broad intent of the speech — is one of four officially listed: to inform, to persuade, to entertain, to inspire.1 A speech reads as one of these whether or not the speaker chose it, because the audience decides how to listen in the first thirty seconds. That decision is the contract:

The general purposeWhat it asks the audience to doWhat a mismatch sounds like
To inform Receive something usable — a fact, a method, an understanding. The audience waits to be moved or persuaded, and hears a lecture.
To persuade Test a claim, and be willing to change position. The audience takes in information and is never asked to agree — while the speaker believes they argued.
To entertain Enjoy the ride, and let the point arrive through it. The anecdotes read as digressions from a serious speech that never starts.
To inspire Be moved, and consider acting. The audience admires the story without being changed; the close feels like sentiment rather than a call.

A mismatch does not look like a broken speech; it looks like resistance — an audience unmoved by perfectly good material. That is why purpose is an evaluator's problem: when the contract is wrong, every other judgement is made through it.

The specific purpose is the sharper instrument. Here is the official definition:

A specific purpose can be expressed as a one-sentence statement about what you hope to accomplish in your speech… It also needs to be worded from the audience's point of view. Clarify exactly what you want the audience to be able to do when you've finished your presentation.

Toastmasters International, Writing a Speech With Purpose (Item 8103), p. 131

Read the clauses as tests. One sentence — if it needs two, the speech is doing two jobs and neither will land. From the audience's point of view — the purpose is a change in them, not a description of your material. Attainable — “make the audience care about orchids” is a wish; nothing the audience does would demonstrate it. Precisely worded — a stranger could read it and know what to watch for. Toastmasters' own model:

The audience will be able to recognize non-threatening fluffy, or cumulus, clouds and identify the difference between those and the more dangerous storm, or cumulonimbus, clouds.

Toastmasters International, Speech Topic and Purpose Worksheet (Item 8057), p. 21

You could evaluate that speech from the back row: by the end you would know whether you could do the thing. That is what a checkable purpose buys you.

Topic is not purpose

The worksheet treats them as separate variables. A speaker picks a topic (“weather”), lists purposes it could serve (“know when to go inside”; “recognize cloud shapes”; “plan your wardrobe”), chooses one, then keeps whichever version of the topic serves it.1 The three layers are different kinds of thing: a topic is a noun, a general purpose is a verb class, and a specific purpose is one sentence describing a change in the audience. The arrow between them can point either way, but they are never the same thing.

A speech can have a clear topic and no purpose at all — which Toastmasters names directly: “you can have a very clear idea of what you want to say without ever considering why you are saying it.”1 The speaker's certainty about the material hides the absence of a point for the listener.

The three questions — and what they become for you

Item 8103 gives the speaker three questions to answer before writing. They are the most compact purpose diagnostic in the official material:

What is the most important thing I want to say? Choose one thing.

Why does it matter to the audience? Consider only the big things.

What do I want people to hold in their hearts and minds long after my speech? Again, one big thing.

Toastmasters International, Writing a Speech With Purpose (Item 8103), p. 111

From your seat, the three questions invert: the speech should be able to tell you what the one thing was, why it mattered, and what you are carrying away. If any of the three has no answer, you have found a purpose fault — and a speech with no destination cannot have had a route.

The purpose test, and the fork it produces

In the minute after the speech, before your notes, finish the sentence: “The speaker wanted me to…” One sentence, from your side of the room, naming a change in you. If it would also fit any other speech on the same topic, you wrote the topic.

Then sort it: the faults need different repairs. Purpose absent: the sentence will not finish at all. Purpose undelivered: you can finish it — it may even be printed on the form — but the speech never got you there. The first is a writing problem; the second is the rest of this lesson.

The easiest check in the whole course

On purpose-focused projects the speaker fills in a Speech Purpose Statement on the form before the speech.1 Read it before they stand up, and the evaluation writes itself: the statement says X — did I receive X? On projects without that field, ask in advance; never grade a speech against a purpose you invented for it.

Section 2Structure: the route

Toastmasters introduces structure with an analogy that is worth taking literally, because it makes the whole section fall into place:

Every speech needs a road map. In planning any kind of a trip, the first step is to determine your destination and plot your route.

Toastmasters International, Fundamentals of Public Speaking, p. 42

Purpose is the destination; structure is the route. Which gives you the standard: a route is never good or bad in the abstract, only in relation to where it is taking you. So the question is never “was this speech well organised?” It is “did the route deliver the destination?” A tidy four-part speech that serves no purpose is badly structured; a loose, digressive story that lands its purpose is well structured.

The official shape is three parts, and each part has a job. The most common structural fault is not a missing part — it is a part doing another part's job.

PartIts jobThe fault when it does a different job
Opening “Catch audience members' attention… spark interest in your topic and lead into the subject of the speech.”2 An opening that summarises has done the conclusion's job, spending the audience's freshest attention on a recap. The four official mistakes — apologetic statement, obvious observation, tedious statement, trite question2 — all spend time without buying attention or direction.
Body “The factual support for your purpose” — statements of fact, statements that support the main idea, and any rebuttal to contrary points of view.2 Material that supports the topic but not the purpose — every fact true, every story good, none of it bearing on what the speech was for. The commonest body fault, and invisible unless you hold the purpose in mind.
Conclusion “The climax; the destination you were reaching for when you started.” Connect back to the opening, and finish with confidence.2 A close that introduces new material is doing the body's job late; a close that apologises abandons the destination. Toastmasters is blunt about the cost: “an inconclusive or apologetic closing can undermine even the best speech.”2

Transitions are the fourth part, and they are the ones you can actually hear. Toastmasters specifies five points at which a bridge is needed: from the introduction to the body; from a main point to a sub-point; from a sub-point to support material; from support material to the next main point; and from the final support material to the conclusion.2 Their official purpose is direct: “these valuable bridges help audiences follow the ideas presented.”1

A listener cannot simply switch sections. When the speaker moves on, the audience is still holding the previous idea. A transition is the instruction that lets them set it down in time. Remove it, and that housekeeping happens silently while the new section is already running, so the audience misses each section's opening sentences, which are usually where the section is named and framed. A missing transition does not merely feel abrupt: it deletes the audience's map, one section-opening at a time.

The parts are jobs, not lengths

A twenty-second opening can do all three of the opening's jobs; a three-minute one can do none. And no rule says a conclusion must summarise — “your closing can include a summary of your main points or a specific call to action.”2 Its job is to resolve, not repeat. Check the parts by function, not by shape, or you will pass a speech that never went anywhere.

Structure is what the audience can hear

The outline is a private plan; the structure is the public route, and the one you evaluate is the second. A speaker can have a flawless outline and an audience that cannot hold the speech — because the transitions were never spoken, the proportion told the wrong story, or the close never collected the opening. A structure finding reports what reached you; it does not accuse anyone of not planning.

Section 3The structures, by name

Item 8103 lists the shapes a speech can take, and the reason an evaluator needs the names is a single contrast. “Your structure didn't serve your purpose” is unactionable. “You used a comparative structure, but the comparison only arrived in the last minute” tells the speaker exactly where to work. The names are a diagnostic vocabulary, and they cost twenty minutes to learn once:

StructureWhat it isIts characteristic failure
Chronological “A sequence of events” in time order.1 The log: everything in order, nothing meaning more than anything else — a timeline instead of a point, unless the last event is what the sequence was building to.
Topical “By topics and subtopics” — major concepts, then smaller and smaller subtopics beneath.1 Categories that overlap or arrive arbitrarily, so the audience cannot predict the kind of thing coming next. Each new topic feels like a new speech.
Spatial “By geography or the physical structure of the topic” — moving across a region or a landscape.1 The itinerary: movement without a claim. A list of locations instead of something about them.
Causal “Link a cause to an effect, or an effect to its cause.”1 The asserted link: the cause is stated rather than shown, or the direction flips halfway and the audience loses the argument's direction.
Comparative “Two or more objects and their shared and/or different attributes.”1 The rigged comparison: no criterion for which attributes matter, or one side gets all the detail.
Problem/Solution “First state the issue and explain its significance. Then propose a feasible and advantageous solution.”1 The unearned solution: the proposal arrives before the audience feels the problem, or it is neither feasible nor advantageous — and the speech argues against itself.
Particular/General/Particular “A specific example that frames broader content… The example given at the beginning… is echoed in the conclusion to help clarify or elucidate your primary message.”1 The open frame: the story is never collected, so the audience holds an example with no general point. The return in the conclusion is the structure; without it there is nothing to hold.

The eighth constraint. Toastmasters lists seven named structures; this course counts an eighth because the general purpose rules some of them out before the speaker chooses. A spatial tour will not persuade; a problem/solution will not entertain; a comparison will not satisfy an audience that came to be inspired. The glossary carries that purpose-driven row as the eighth — a constraint on the other seven, not another shape beside them. The seven names are Toastmasters'; the count of eight is this course's.

Two things to hold. First, most speeches combine structures — a chronological story inside a problem/solution frame is one speech, not two — so name the dominant one and treat the rest as texture. Second, the names describe what the audience received, not what the speaker wrote down. A speaker may have planned a topical speech and delivered something the room heard as a list of stories. Report the second.

Why the purpose comes first in the diagnosis

You cannot judge a structure without a purpose to judge it against. Change “the history of the locomotive” from to inform to to persuade us that trains built the modern world, and the same chronological shape stops being neutral: the sequence either builds the argument or it does not. That is why the 8103 rubric scores the two together.1

Section 4Deliberate or accidental

You never see the outline. You hear the speech once, in real time, and afterwards you have to decide whether a structure was there. Five things you can check, all of them from your seat:

1. The shape test

After the speech, before your notes: can you name the structure? “That was a problem/solution.” “That was chronological.” If the only sentence you can produce is “she talked about her job, then her mother, then some advice,” no shape reached you. The speech may have had a perfect outline; what it lacked was a structure the audience could hear. This course calls it the shape test; the name is a teaching device, not official vocabulary.

2. Predictability

At the end of each section, could you guess the kind of thing coming next? Not the content — the category. Deliberate structure makes the next move predictable in type even when the content surprises; accidental structure changes type without warning — a story becomes a statistic becomes a joke becomes a warning. Every jump forces the audience to re-orient, and each re-orientation costs the next section's first sentence.

3. Proportion

Where did the minutes go? Time is the audience's only measure of importance. If the point the speech existed for got ninety seconds and the context got four minutes, the audience learned — correctly — that the context was the point. Lesson 02 called this proportion; here it is the route's arithmetic, and speakers rarely notice it, because the longest section is simply the one they most enjoyed preparing.

4. The return

Did the close collect the opening? “Always connect your opening to your conclusion,”2 and the reason is mechanical: an image, a question or a story at the start is a promise, and the conclusion is where it is kept. Under a particular/general/particular structure the return is the structure: the opening example comes back, and the general point is made by the fact that it does. A speech that never closes its frame leaves the audience holding a story and waiting for the point.

5. The joints

Could you hear the transitions at the points where two sections met? A real bridge names the move — “that's the why; here's the how,” or 8103's own example, “now, let me take you to a mid-century farm outside Dublin”1 — and it does what “so” does not: it tells the audience to put down what they were carrying. Without it they are still carrying the old section when the new one begins, and the new section's name goes past them. A speech with no audible joints is a series of arrivals nobody announced.

What accidental structure feels like from the audience

You remember the pieces, not the path. You can retell the anecdote but not the argument, and you cannot say which of two sections was the point. And the speech ends before you expected it to — the moment that tells you the conclusion was never a destination. Any of these is a legitimate finding, anchored to the moment it happened, and none requires you to know what the speaker planned.

Section 5From fault to finding

Purpose and structure are the largest findings available in a speech, and the easiest to report badly, because the vocabulary for them is vague. “It was a bit disorganised” and “you need a clearer purpose” are true of hundreds of speeches and useful in none. The rule is Lesson 01's: be specific — if the organisation confused you, “say so but clearly address what confused you and offer a suggestion for improvement.”3 And the companion rule: “every comment in which you reveal a weakness should also include a suggestion for overcoming that weakness.”2

The fork comes first. Three different faults produce the same feeling in the room — that didn't quite work — and they need opposite repairs.

What you receivedThe faultWhat actually fixes it
“I could not finish the sentence the speaker wanted me to…” Purpose absent. Write the purpose before the next draft, from the audience's point of view, and use it as the filter.
“I knew what it was for — it was on the form — but the speech never got me there.” Purpose undelivered. Build the route: choose a structure that fits the purpose, and put the minutes where the purpose is.
“I could follow each section, and I could not hold the whole.” Structure fault. Fix the joints: name the moves, cut or re-order the sections, re-budget the time.

The order of repair puts purpose first for a reason you can now see. An undelivered purpose is repaired by structure; an absent one cannot be repaired by any structure, because there is no standard to build against — which is why a speaker who writes the purpose statement often finds the structure repairs itself. Filtering turns a pile of material into a route.

The five-step content diagnosis

Run this in the minute after a speech, before you read your own notes.

  1. Finish the purpose sentence. “The speaker wanted me to…” from the audience's point of view. If it will not finish, that is the largest finding in the room, and it goes first.
  2. Write the route. Three or four markers, in order, as you heard them. If you need your notes, the shape did not reach you in real time — itself a finding.
  3. Name the structure. The shape test. “That was a problem/solution,” or “I could not name one.”
  4. Find the anchor. The moment the route went invisible: the joint you had to guess, the section that surprised you, the point that got ninety seconds — your WHAT and WHEN.
  5. Choose the fork, then say it with the three coordinates — behaviour, location, effect on you — and a recommendation for the next presentation.

Phrase patterns for content findings

Never saySay instead
You need a clearer purpose. I could tell you every topic you covered, and I still could not finish the sentence “you wanted me to…”.
It was disorganised. I was still carrying your second point when the third began, so I missed its opening. “That's the why; here's the how” would have carried me across.
Your ending came out of nowhere. I was still waiting for the idea to resolve when you said thank you. The speech stopped rather than landed.
You should have used a timeline. You told it as three separate lessons, and by the third I had lost which one was the point. Told as a sequence — what you tried, what happened, what you learned — the last one would have arrived as the conclusion, not one more item.
Name the strength the same way you name the fault

“Great structure” cannot be repeated, because it does not say what worked. “You used a problem/solution shape, and the solution arrived exactly when I had started to feel the problem — I was ready for it rather than being sold at” names the choice to keep making. A strength named precisely is a behaviour to repeat; a vague one is a compliment they will forget.

DrillsSolidify it

Reading this section produces fluency — the feeling that you understand it. The drills produce storage strength: the ability to run a purpose-and-structure diagnosis on a speech you have never heard, while the room is watching. Do them in order, and do not skip the recall.

Drill 1 · Retrieval practice Recall the instruments

Close the page. Do not scroll up. From memory, write down:

  1. The three layers — topic, general purpose, specific purpose — and the official test for a purpose statement.
  2. The four general purposes, and what each one commits the audience to do.
  3. The three jobs of the opening, and the four official opening mistakes.
  4. The five points at which a transition is needed.
  5. The seven named organisational structures.
  6. The five evidence checks for a deliberate structure.

The effort of failing to recall is the mechanism, not a sign that you learned nothing. Sit with a blank for thirty seconds before you scroll.

Autosaves on this device.

1. The three layers

Topic — the subject matter; a noun.
General purpose — the broad intent: inform, persuade, entertain or inspire; the listening contract, and it exists whether chosen or not.
Specific purpose — one sentence, precisely worded, attainable, and written from the audience's point of view: what they will be able to do when you finish. It is both the standard for relevance and the destination for the conclusion.

Test: could you tell, from your seat, whether it happened? A statement that would fit any speech on the topic is a topic, not a purpose.

2. The four general purposes

To inform — receive something usable. To persuade — test a claim and be willing to change position. To entertain — enjoy the ride and let the point arrive through it. To inspire — be moved, and consider acting.

3. The opening, and its four mistakes

Jobs: catch attention, spark interest in the topic, lead into the subject. Mistakes: an apologetic statement; a general or obvious observation; a long or tedious statement; a trite question.

Body: the factual support for the purpose — statements of fact, support for the main idea, rebuttal of contrary views. Conclusion: the climax, the destination; connect back to the opening and finish with confidence.

4. The five transition points

Introduction → body · main point → sub-point · sub-point → support material · support material → next main point · final support material → conclusion.

5. The seven structures

Chronological · Topical · Spatial · Causal · Comparative · Problem/Solution · Particular/General/Particular. (The eighth in this course is the general-purpose constraint on the other seven, not another shape.)

6. The five evidence checks

The shape test — can you name the structure afterwards? Predictability — could you guess the kind of the next section? Proportion — did the minutes go where the purpose was? The return — did the close collect the opening? The joints — could you hear the transitions?

Drill 2 · Discrimination Name the shape, find the fault

The first seven items are Toastmasters' own exercises from Item 8103, pages 7–8 — each describes a speech and asks for its best structure. The answers are the official ones. The last five are symptoms a real evaluator might notice; decide whether the primary fault is purpose or structure. You get immediate feedback on each. Aim for 12 out of 12 — and read the explanation even when you are right.

“Marie feels her club could do more to reach guests. She wants to start an email list for potential new members, and will present the idea to club members.”

“Tomasz wants to speak about the impact a positive attitude can have, using one day in his office with a positive colleague as his example.”

“Peter wants to present dog breeds from three groups — hound, working and sporting — with examples from each group.”

“Saif wants to share the history of the locomotive: its invention, how it changed the world, and its modern-day role.”

“Zhang wants to tell his audience that regular exercise contributes to better overall health.”

“Sonia wants to present the similarities and differences between the economic structures of two countries.”

“Francesco wants to talk about climbing the world's tallest mountain, and what happens to the body at different levels of elevation.”

“I could tell you every topic she covered, and I still could not finish the sentence ‘she wanted me to…’.”

“Every time she moved to a new point I was still holding the last one, and I missed the first sentence of each new section.”

“Four minutes on the diet chapter, forty seconds on the training method — in a speech about how to train a puppy.”

“He opened with the avalanche story and never came back to it; the speech ended on a list of equipment.”

“The audience came expecting information and got a sales pitch. Several people stopped listening before the pitch started.”

Drill 3 · Production Rewrite the vague comment

These are the five comments about content that get nods in every club and help nobody. For each one, write the version you would actually say: the fault, the moment, the effect on you, and a recommendation for the next presentation. Then reveal the model answer and score yourself against the checklist.

Comment 1

“You need a clearer purpose.”

Model answer

What is wrong with the comment: it names a fix before the diagnosis, it cannot be acted on (“clearer” how?), and it may be false — the purpose may have been clear and simply never delivered. Section 5's fork has to come first.

If the purpose was absent: “I could tell you every topic you covered — the apprenticeship, the move, the first shop — and I still could not finish the sentence ‘you wanted me to…’. The speech seemed to be built from the material out toward the room, so nothing was off-topic and nothing got cut. Writing the purpose in one sentence from my side of the room — ‘by the end, the audience will be able to…’ — would give you the filter for the next draft.”

If the purpose was undelivered: “Your purpose statement says you wanted me to be able to recognise the three warning signs. I can name them, but only because I wrote them down — in the speech they arrived in the last forty seconds with no support. Moving them earlier, and giving each one a moment of its own, would have got me there.”

WHAT the missing purpose, or the unsupported one · WHEN the close, where the destination should have been · SO WHAT “I could not finish the sentence ‘you wanted me to…’” / “I only knew the signs because I wrote them down”

Comment 2

“It was a bit disorganised.”

Model answer

What is wrong with the comment: “disorganised” is a verdict about the whole speech, and a whole speech is not something the speaker can act on. It also tells them nothing about which joint failed or whether the order itself was the problem.

Say: “I was with you through the second point. Then the third began without a signal, so I was still carrying the second one and I missed the opening of the third. One sentence — ‘that's the why; here's the how’ — would have let me put the second point down in time. If the order itself was the issue, tell me why the third came after the second; from the outside, either order would have worked and I could not tell what the sequence was doing.”

WHAT a missing transition between the second and third points · WHEN the move into the third point · SO WHAT “I missed the opening of the third”

Comment 3

“The ending came out of nowhere.”

Model answer

Two candidates, and they need opposite fixes. Structure — the return: the speech had a destination, and the close failed to collect the opening's promise. Purpose — absent: there was never a destination, so no close could have arrived anywhere. “Out of nowhere” is the feeling in both cases; the diagnosis is whether you knew where you were going.

If the return failed: “You opened with the image of your grandmother's kitchen, and I was holding it for the whole speech, waiting to come back to it. When you closed on the statistics instead, I was left with the image and no resolution. A last line back in that kitchen — even eight words — would have closed the circle.”

If the purpose was absent: “I was still waiting for the idea to resolve when you said thank you. The speech stopped rather than landed. Deciding, before the next draft, what you want me to be able to do when you finish, gives the close somewhere to arrive.”

WHAT an uncollected opening, or an unresolved idea · WHEN the final line, at “thank you” · SO WHAT “I was left with the image and no resolution” / “the speech stopped rather than landed”

Comment 4

“Your structure was great.”

Model answer

What is wrong with the comment: it is a compliment, not information. The speaker cannot repeat “great”, and they cannot tell which of their choices produced it. A strength named precisely is a behaviour they can keep; a vague strength is something they will thank you for and forget.

Say: “You used a problem/solution shape, and the solution arrived exactly when I had started to feel the problem — I was ready for it rather than being sold at. That is the moment the speech turned from information into something I wanted to act on, and it happened because of where you put the turn. Keep building your speeches around the point where the audience is ready.”

WHAT a problem/solution structure with the solution placed after the audience feels the problem · WHEN the turn, around two-thirds of the way in · SO WHAT “I was ready for it rather than being sold at”

Comment 5

“You should have used a timeline.”

Model answer

What is wrong with the comment: the advice habit has returned — an opinion about what to do, issued before the observation. Worse, you have not named the structure the speaker did use, so the recommendation floats free of any evidence. And a structure cannot be recommended until it is tested against the purpose: a timeline serves a story, but it may bury a persuasion.

Say: “You told it as three separate lessons, and by the third I had lost which one was the point — each arrived with the same weight, so none of them was the destination. If they had been laid out as a sequence — what you tried, what happened, what you learned — the third would have arrived as the end of a story rather than as one more item. I am not certain a timeline is what you want, but something that makes one of the three the destination would have served your purpose better.”

WHAT a topical list of three lessons, all weighted equally · WHEN the third lesson, which the audience had no reason to hear as the point · SO WHAT “I had lost which one was the point”

Note the shape of the recommendation: offered as a possibility, with the effect stated, and tied back to the purpose. That is Lesson 01's rule, applied to structure.

Score each of your five rewrites

  • Did you name the fault before the fix? Purpose absent, purpose undelivered, or structure — the fork decides which repair the speaker needs.
  • Did you name the structure, or say you could not? “Disorganised” is not a structure. “A topical list where every item weighed the same” is.
  • Does your sentence contain WHAT, WHEN and SO WHAT? Content findings are the easiest to leave unanchored, because the fault is in the whole speech rather than at one moment. Find the moment anyway.
  • Did you tie the judgement back to the purpose? Structure is only good or bad in relation to a destination. If your comment would apply to any speech on the topic, it is a verdict about tidiness, not about the route.
  • Did you skip the fix? Every weakness comment needs a suggestion for overcoming it — offered as a possibility, with the effect stated.
  • Did you make the strength repeatable? If you rewrote Comment 4, your version should tell the speaker exactly which choice to make again.

Two or more checks failed on any item? Rewrite that item now. The repetition is the point.

Drill 4 · Field work Chart one real speech

This is where the lesson leaves the page. At your next club meeting — the next one, not “sometime” — pick one speech and do two things at once. First, listen for the joins: press Transition every time you hear a move being signalled, and No transition every time the speech changes direction without one. Do not deliberate; press whichever it feels like.

0 Transition
0 No transition

Second, in the minute after the speech ends — before you look at your counts, and before you read any notes — answer the four prompts below. These are the raw materials of a real evaluation.

Prompt 1

Finish the sentence, from your side of the room: “The speaker wanted me to…”

Strong answer

It names a change in you, not a topic: “to be able to spot the two warning signs on my own router,” not “to know about home networks.” If the sentence would fit any speech on the topic, you wrote the topic — try again before you accept it.

If you genuinely cannot finish it, that is the answer, and it is the largest finding in the room. Write “I could not finish this sentence” rather than leaving it blank.

Prompt 2

The route, in three or four markers, as you heard it.

Strong answer

Markers, not sentences — the section names as the audience received them. If you needed your notes to reconstruct this, mark it: the route only became visible afterwards. That is a finding about the speech's audibility, not evidence that you were daydreaming.

Prompt 3

The structure, by name — or “I could not name one.”

Strong answer

One of the seven, plus any structure it was nested inside: “a chronological story inside a problem/solution frame.” If you cannot name one, write that — the shape test failing is a legitimate result and worth reporting to the speaker carefully, as what you received rather than what they failed to plan.

Prompt 4

The moment the route went invisible — or the moment it worked.

Strong answer

A moment, not an impression: “when the budget section began at about four minutes, with no bridge — I was still holding the staffing point and missed its first sentence.” Or the positive version: “the line ‘that's the why; here's the how’ at three minutes reset me completely, and the second half landed cleanly.” Either one is ready to become an anchored finding.

If the purpose sentence would not finish

You have found a purpose-absent fault, and it goes first in your evaluation — before any structure comment. Everything else in the speech was being judged against a destination you never received.

If you could name the route but not the structure

The speech had direction without a shape. That is still a finding, and the fix is usually in the joints and the signposting rather than in a rewrite: the speaker knows where they are going, and the audience needs to be told.

If you needed your notes to reconstruct the route

The shape did not reach you in real time. Ask whether your counts agree: a speech with many transitions and a route you still could not reconstruct may have had transitions that announced moves without naming them — “so, anyway” is not a bridge.

Reading the tally

Most beginners undercount transitions on the first attempt, because they are listening to the content rather than to the joins. A second pass over the same speech — or the next speech you watch — normally hears two or three times as many. The ratio matters less than the pattern: transitions clustered at every joint is a deliberate route; long runs of “no transition” between short, well-bridged sections is a speech whose shape lives in its opening and close but not in the middle.

The corrective exercise

On your next evaluation, take the purpose sentence and the route you wrote here and deliver them in your evaluation as your first content comment: “Here is what I understood you wanted me to be able to do, and here is the route I followed.” The speaker will tell you immediately whether you received the speech they wrote — and that answer is worth more than any score.
