Lesson 01 · Foundations

You Are Not a Judge

What an evaluation actually is, and the one habit that separates a helpful evaluator from an annoying one.

Mission: evaluate speeches at Toastmasters Time: ~20 min reading + ~25 min drills Prerequisite: none

The moment you are handed the evaluation form, a quiet pressure arrives. Someone has spent two weeks on this speech, and they have just handed you a pen and said tell me the truth. Almost every new evaluator responds to that pressure by trying to sound like an expert. That instinct is the single largest source of bad evaluations.

Section 1What the role actually is

Toastmasters is unusually blunt about this. The organisation's own guide to evaluating opens by drawing a hard line around what you are and are not:

Your purpose as an evaluator is to provide honest reaction in a constructive manner to the person's efforts… You are not a judge or an authority on speaking or leadership. When you evaluate, you are simply giving your own reaction to the person's speaking or leadership efforts. An evaluation is an opinion, nothing more.

Toastmasters International, Effective Evaluation (Item 202), p. 31

Read that again, because it is doing more work than it first appears. Three separate consequences fall out of it.

1. You are not trying to be right. You are trying to be useful.

There is no correct evaluation to get wrong. There is only a reaction that helps the speaker see something they could not see, and a reaction that does not. This is liberating: it means you never have to perform expertise. You only have to be accurate about what happened inside you while they spoke.

And you are uniquely positioned to report that. A speaker has never watched themselves speak. They have felt themselves speak, which is a completely different sensation — the rush of adrenaline, the paragraph they nearly forgot, the moment the room went quiet. What they cannot access is what the room saw. Their hands. Their pace. The six seconds where their eyes went to the ceiling. You can. That is the entire value you bring.

2. You cannot change them, and you are not responsible for trying.

The guide is explicit: you "cannot change the person's behavior or force the person to accept your ideas and suggested improvements."1 Nor can you demand they repeat a project. The decision to accept your suggestions belongs to the speaker alone.

New evaluators find this frustrating. Experienced ones find it clarifying — it removes the burden of being responsible for someone else's improvement, and replaces it with responsibility for the quality of your observation.

3. You are not speaking for the room.

Your evaluation is one opinion. The guide warns that "your opinions may differ from those of the rest of the audience."1 This has an immediate, concrete linguistic consequence we will come to in Section 3.

The two ways an evaluation fails

An evaluation has exactly two failure modes, and they are opposite:

“A harsh evaluation may cause a member to leave the club. An overly kind evaluation may not help the member to improve, making the member frustrated and unhappy.”1

Notice what this means. Being nice is also a failure. An evaluation that flatters is not a safe default — it is a different way of wasting the speaker's fortnight. The goal is not kindness. The goal is honest reaction, constructively delivered. Kindness is how you deliver it, not what you deliver.

Section 2The three ingredients every evaluation needs

Strip away style, length and experience, and every useful evaluation contains three things. Even a thirty-second evaluation of a table topic needs all three.

1
The effect it had on you

Not what they did — what happened in you as a result. “I followed every word.” “I stopped listening at the truck story.” This is the most valuable thing you can report, because it is pure information the speaker has no access to.

2
What they did well

Specific and observed. Not “great speech” but “when you described the fudge cake, my mouth watered.”1 The speaker needs to know which behaviours to keep. Most evaluators under-invest here and it is a serious loss — a strength you do not name is a strength they might drop.

3
Where they could improve — plus a specific recommendation

This is two things, and the second is the one people skip. An observation without a suggestion is a complaint. “When you were talking about the truck, I wasn't sure if you were referring to the new one or the old one” is an observation. “Giving each truck a nickname and using it throughout would have made the references clearer to me — and maybe added humour” is a recommendation.1

Why ingredient 3 must be two things

A complaint tells the speaker that something is broken and leaves them to guess at the repair. That is the opposite of help — it hands them a problem and removes the information they need to solve it. Notice also that the model recommendation above is phrased as a possibility with a stated effect: “may have worked better… would have made the references clearer to me.” It is offered, not imposed. That phrasing is not politeness for its own sake — it preserves the speaker's ownership of the decision, which is exactly what makes them act on it.

Section 3The one habit: give feedback, not advice

If you take a single behaviour away from this lesson, take this one. It is the highest-leverage distinction in the entire discipline, and it is stated with unusual precision in the Pathways guide:

Feedback is information, while advice is an opinion. The evaluator gives the speaker information about what they observed. This helps the speaker to learn how they are being perceived.

Toastmasters International, Evaluation and Feedback (Item 8100), p. 92

The official guide pairs them side by side, and the difference is smaller and more consequential than most people expect:

Advice — an opinionFeedback — information
Don't be so scared.I noticed that you were shaking.
You need to stand up straight.I observed that your upper body was tilted forward.
You have to speak up.I had trouble hearing you.
You need to improve your eye contact.I saw that you were averting your eyes from the audience.
You didn't move enough.I noticed you remained still and in one place when you spoke.

Look at the left column honestly. Every line is probably true. Every line is also something the speaker already suspects, cannot verify, and has no way to act on precisely. Now read the right column the same way. Every line gives them something they did not have.

Why feedback beats advice — three mechanisms

(a) Advice smuggles in a diagnosis that may be wrong

“Don't be so scared” presumes three things at once: that the speaker chose to be scared, that fear was the cause of what you saw, and that you know what the alternative looks like for them. Any of those may be false. What if they were cold? What if they had just sprinted up a flight of stairs? What if the trembling was an adrenaline response they cannot switch off and know perfectly well about?

Feedback makes no such claim. “I noticed that you were shaking” is a statement about your observation, and it is very hard to be wrong about your own observation. You are reporting data. Advice is a conclusion drawn from data — and the speaker is better placed than you to draw it, because they know their own history, their own body, and the specific thing they were working on.

(b) Feedback leaves the speaker holding the wheel

This is the mechanism that actually produces change. The guide's instruction to speakers is that applying feedback is their responsibility, and it explicitly protects their right to choose differently from you: “Meeting your personal goals, even if those goals differ from the goals your evaluator laid out for you, is your prerogative.”2

Advice collapses that. It says: here is the fix. The speaker's options become comply or reject, and rejection is cheap when the fix is someone else's. Feedback leaves the gap between observation and remedy deliberately open, and that gap is where the speaker's own decision lives. A decision the speaker made is a decision the speaker will act on.

There is a second-order benefit. The guide asks evaluators to limit suggestions to two or three points, because “too much information adds stress and may dissuade the speaker from trying again.”2 Feedback, being shorter and non-prescriptive, naturally crowds less.

(c) Feedback cannot be argued with

This is the mechanism that will save you as a beginner. Suppose you tell a speaker “you need to speak up,” and it turns out they were perfectly audible to everyone but you. You are now in a dispute you cannot win, and the speaker has a reason to discard your whole evaluation.

Now suppose you said “I had trouble hearing you.” That is unfalsifiably true. It happened. Even the person who heard them perfectly well cannot contradict you. You have made a claim that costs you nothing to be wrong about, and the speaker can still take the useful part — someone in this room had trouble, that is worth knowing.

The self-test for any comment you are about to make

Could the speaker reasonably reply “no I didn't”? If yes, you have written advice or a judgement, and you should rewrite it. “I had trouble hearing you” cannot be answered with “no you didn't.” “You need to speak up” can.

Put it in time: what makes feedback checkable

There is one more test, and it catches comments that pass everything above. “I noticed you were shaking” is feedback, not advice. It reports your observation, it stays on the behaviour, and it cannot be answered with “no I wasn't.” The speaker can still do nothing with it — because it has no location.

A behaviour without a when cannot be checked, cannot be rehearsed, and cannot be told apart from a habit. Was it one moment or the whole six minutes? The speaker has no way to look. Toastmasters' own model example already supplies the answer. Its illustration of good feedback is not “the references were unclear.” It is: “When you were talking about the truck, I wasn't sure if you were referring to the new one or the old one.”1 The organisation's standing instruction is simply “Be specific.”1

The anchor test

Could the speaker rewind to that exact moment and watch it?

If yes, you have a finding. If no, you have an impression — label it as one (“I don't have a specific moment for this, so take it for what it's worth”) or hold it back. Never invent a location: a false “during your second point” sends the speaker looking for something that was not there, and they will start to distrust your accurate observations too.

Feedback that survives the anchor test names three things. This course calls them the three coordinates, and calls the location the anchor. Neither term is Toastmasters vocabulary — the underlying rule (“be specific”) is theirs; the framing is a teaching device built to make it operational.

CoordinateWhat it isExample
WHAT The behaviour — observable, not a trait. “Your hands were shaking” — not “you were nervous.”
WHEN The location in the speech. The coordinate people omit. “through the opening — it had settled by the second point.”
SO WHAT The effect it had on you as a listener. “and I was watching your hands instead of hearing your first line.”

WHEN carries the most weight. It is what moves a comment from “about the performance” to “about a specific moment the speaker can act on,” and it is what lets them verify your claim themselves rather than take your word for it. Counting matters too, and it changes what you should say: once is possibly an accident and usually worth leaving alone; two or three times is an emerging pattern worth naming; throughout is a default behaviour — the highest-value thing you can report, because the speaker cannot feel their own default.

The sentence starters to use

The guide recommends “words that describe your own reactions to the speaker,” and gives the phrasings it wants to hear:1

  • “I was impressed with…”
  • “I was confused about…”
  • “When I heard…”
  • “I think the speech's purpose would have been clearer if…”
  • “I liked it when…”

And from the Pathways guide, the rule that generates all of them: “I” messages keep the focus on behaviours, not the person. The guide draws the contrast sharply:

“You were inspiring,” is about the person. “I observed how passionately and energetically you inspired the audience with your personal story,” is about the behaviour.

Toastmasters International, Evaluation and Feedback (Item 8100), p. 122

That example is worth studying, because the revision is not just longer — it is evidence-bearing. “You were inspiring” asks the speaker to accept a verdict. The revised version tells them exactly which behaviour produced which effect, which means it can be repeated on purpose.

The words to strike from your vocabulary

The guide supplies an explicit blacklist:1

BannedWhy it fails
“we think”, “we believe”, “the audience would have”, “the audience didn't understand” You are not the audience. These imply you speak for others and immediately invite disagreement from anyone who felt differently.
“they say”, “one must”, “people are” Impersonal phrasing that hides who is actually making the claim — and implies the evaluation is aimed at someone other than the person in front of you.
“good leaders don't”, “that was the wrong thing to say”, “if you want to do it right, you must” Judgement words. They declare a universal standard and put the speaker on trial against it.
“you did”, “you were” Describes the person rather than the behaviour, which is exactly what triggers defensiveness — and defensiveness is fatal to learning.
“never”, “always” Exaggerations that “detract from your message.” One instance is not a pattern, and an inaccurate claim gives the speaker licence to ignore the accurate part.

One final rule, and it is easy to break without noticing: do not repeat a point once you have made it. The guide's reason is precise — repetition “can sound like nagging.”1 Say it once, clearly, then move on.

Section 4The shape: Commend → Recommend → Commend

Almost everyone has heard of the “feedback sandwich.” Toastmasters' own version is more precisely named Commend – Recommend – Commend (CRC), and understanding the sequence matters more than the metaphor.3

MoveWhat it does
Commend The speaker relaxes. Their guard comes down and their ability to hear the next sentence goes up.
Recommend Delivered while attention is at its highest, and framed as information rather than verdict.
Commend Establishes what they walk away feeling about the whole experience. This is the part they will remember on the drive home.

The reason this ordering works is not politeness. It is that a defensive listener cannot learn. The left-hand column of the advice table above does not merely fail to inform — it actively switches off the mechanism that would let the speaker use anything else you say. Sequencing is a technical requirement for the information to land, the same way attention to breath is a technical requirement for projecting your voice.

The honest caveat about the sandwich

The sandwich has a well-documented weakness: delivered mechanically, it becomes recognisable as a formula, and the audience — including the speaker — can see the bread coming. Toastmasters Magazine has published pieces arguing against the “sandwich of affirmation” precisely because ritual replaces substance.4

The fix is not to abandon the shape. It is to make the commendations real. “Great job!” is bread. “I noticed your pace dropped noticeably when you got to your father's letter, and the room went still” is a commendation the speaker can actually use as a model.

What actually matters, underneath the shape, is the ratio. The guide's instruction is to “seek a balance that favors positive remarks over negative.”2 CRC is one way of achieving that balance. It is not the only one, and real evaluations are not all 1–1–1.

Why you must finish well

One line in the guide is worth committing to memory, because it is the standard against which your evaluation should be judged:

The speaker or leader should always walk away from the meeting feeling motivated and eager to begin working on his next project. How you finish your evaluation often determines whether a speaker is motivated or unmotivated.

Toastmasters International, Effective Evaluation (Item 202), p. 51

This is not sentimentality. A motivated speaker practises, and practice is the only thing that produces improvement. An evaluation that is accurate but demoralising fails the same way a flattering one does: it produces no next attempt worth judging.

Section 5Where this sits on the ladder

Everything in this lesson is the floor, not the ceiling. Correct feedback language and a defensible shape make your evaluation useful — what Toastmasters would score as a solid 3, “Accomplished.”

The top of the ladder is defined by a different document entirely: the Evaluation Contest Judge's Guide, which scores evaluations out of 100 across four criteria. Two of those four — Analytical Quality (40 points) and Recommendations (30 points) — are about the substance of what you saw, and you cannot earn them until you know what to look for. That is what the next several lessons build.

DrillsSolidify it

Reading this section will give you fluency — the feeling that you understand it. The drills below are what create storage strength: the ability to do it under pressure, in a real meeting, with your heart going. Do them in order. Do not skip the recall drill.

Drill 1 · Retrieval practice Recall before you reveal

Close the page. Do not scroll up. From memory, write down:

  1. The three ingredients every evaluation needs.
  2. Five sentence starters that keep the focus on behaviour.
  3. The self-test that tells you whether a comment is feedback or advice.
  4. The anchor test, and the three coordinates.

The effort of failing to recall is what builds memory. If you draw a blank, sit with it for thirty seconds before scrolling — that discomfort is the mechanism working.

Drill 2 · Discrimination Advice or feedback?

Twelve real-sounding evaluator comments. Six are advice (an opinion about what the speaker should do). Six are feedback (information about what the evaluator observed). You get immediate feedback on each one. Aim for 12 out of 12 before you move on — and read the explanation even when you get it right.

“I noticed you were shaking at the start.”

“You need to make more eye contact.”

“You have to speak up.”

“I had trouble hearing you during the last third.”

“I observed that your upper body was tilted forward.”

“Don't be so scared next time.”

“I lost the thread when you moved from your second point to your third.”

“You didn't move enough.”

“I noticed you stayed in one place while you spoke.”

“You should stand up straighter.”

“I saw that you were looking over the audience's heads.”

“You need to cut your opening down.”

Drill 3 · Production Rewrite advice as feedback

Recognising the difference is not the skill. Producing feedback is the skill. Below are five pieces of advice. Write a feedback version of each — then reveal the model answers and score yourself against the checklist.

Advice 1

“You need to relax.”

Model answer

“I noticed your shoulders were up around your ears and you were gripping the lectern for the first minute. It stopped after that — and that is when I started hearing the words instead of watching you.”

WHAT raised shoulders, gripping the lectern · WHEN the first minute · SO WHAT “I started hearing the words instead of watching you”

Advice 2

“You should smile more.”

Model answer

“I noticed the room warm up the moment you smiled during the story about your daughter. Up to then I had been reading you as serious.”

WHAT a smile · WHEN during the daughter story · SO WHAT “the room warm up” / “I had been reading you as serious”

Advice 3

“Your conclusion was weak.”

Model answer

“I felt the speech stop rather than land. I was still waiting for the idea to resolve when you said thank you, so I am not sure what you wanted me to leave with.”

WHAT the speech stopping rather than landing · WHEN the conclusion, at “thank you” · SO WHAT “I am not sure what you wanted me to leave with”

Advice 4

“You have to stop saying ‘um’.”

Model answer

“I counted about eleven ‘um’s in the first two minutes. By the fourth one I was listening for the next one instead of following your story.”

WHAT filler words, counted · WHEN the first two minutes · SO WHAT “I was listening for the next one instead of following your story”

Advice 5

“You need better vocal variety.”

Model answer

“In the last third your voice stayed at one pitch and one speed, and I stopped hearing the individual sentences. The section about your mother was where I lost it.”

WHAT one pitch, one speed · WHEN the last third, specifically the section about your mother · SO WHAT “I stopped hearing the individual sentences”

Score each of your five rewrites

  • Does it start with “I”? If not, you drifted back into describing the person.
  • Could the speaker reply “no I didn't”? If yes, rewrite it. “You didn't relax” can be argued with. “Your shoulders were up” cannot.
  • Does it answer WHEN? Could the speaker rewind to that exact moment? If not, you have an impression — label it as one or drop it.
  • Does it answer SO WHAT? What did the behaviour do to you? A sentence that stops at the behaviour is an inspection report.
  • Did you skip the fix? If you caught yourself writing “so next time you should…”, that is the advice habit returning. The fix belongs in the recommendation, and the recommendation comes after the observation.

Two or more checks failed on any item? Rewrite that item now, before you do Drill 4. The repetition is the point.

Drill 4 · Field work Tally a real evaluator

This is where the lesson leaves the page. At your next club meeting — the next one, not “sometime” — listen to one evaluator and count. Every time they say something that is information about what they observed, press Feedback. Every time they say something that is an opinion about what the speaker should do, press Advice.

Do not try to judge whether the evaluation was good. Just count. You will learn two things at once: how quickly your ear develops, and how much advice is in the average evaluation — which is usually more than anyone expects.

As you listen, notice also which comments name a moment in the speech — an anchor — and which could have been said about any speech at all. That distinction is the difference between a finding and an impression.

0 Feedback
0 Advice

Your counts are saved on this device, so you can keep tallying across several meetings. Bring the numbers to your next session with me — they tell us a lot about what to work on next.

Once you have your count, answer one question: of the advice you heard, how much do you think the speaker could actually have acted on without asking a follow-up question? That question is the reason this course exists.
