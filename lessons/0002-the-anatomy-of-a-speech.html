Lesson 02 · Foundations

The Anatomy of a Speech

The map of everything you are judging — content and delivery — and why the split decides what you say first.

Mission: name every criterion, and what each one adds Time: ~20 min reading + ~30 min drills Prerequisite: Lesson 01

An audience member listens for meaning. You are listening for parts. That single difference is the job — and it means the first thing an evaluator needs is not an opinion. It is a map: a fixed list of what there is to see, so that what you report is what you looked for rather than what happened to catch your eye.

Section 1The form is already a map

Toastmasters does not hand you a blank page and ask for your feelings about a speech. It hands you a form with a defined list of criteria on it, and one sentence explaining what the form is for:

Read the evaluation guide for the project. The guide explains what you should be looking for as you evaluate. It lists specific questions about the speaker or leader and provides a space in which you may write comments… You will also use this guide as a basis for your verbal evaluation.

Toastmasters International, Effective Evaluation (Item 202), p. 41

Every Level 1 speech project uses the same seven core criteria, and they are the spine of the whole discipline:2

CriterionThe form's own wording
ClaritySpoken language is clear and is easily understood
Vocal VarietyUses tone, speed, and volume as tools

Projects focused on one skill add criteria on top — Specific Speech Purpose and General Speech Purpose (Writing a Speech With Purpose), Purposeful Movement and Unintentional Movement (Introduction to Vocal Variety and Body Language), Applied Feedback (the second speech in Evaluation and Feedback). Every level of every criterion is defined in Toastmasters' own words, transcribed in The Excellence Ladder.

So the map is printed for you. It buys two things.

1. You see what you look for

A six-minute speech contains far more events than any listener can register, so what you notice is never a sample of all of it — it is the subset you were watching for. Without a list, that subset is whatever was loudest: a gesture you liked, a filler word that grated, the moment you got lost. With the list, you can place your attention deliberately, and you can tell what you failed to check.

2. You stop confusing “noticeable” with “important”

A gesture repeated eleven times is easy to see and small in effect. A speech with no purpose is hard to notice and enormous in effect. The map does not tell you which is which — the rest of this lesson does — but it establishes that both exist, and it gives you the vocabulary the speaker's own project is already using.

It also comes with an official warning about how the map goes wrong:

Good eye contact, meaningful, natural gestures, and correct grammar contribute to the overall effect of a speech but should not be given so much emphasis that they detract from the basic purpose of the evaluation.

Toastmasters International, Effective Evaluation (Item 202), p. 41

Read that as a statement about proportion, not about eye contact. The mechanical criteria are the ones you can watch happening, so they are the ones that will fill your notes. Most of this lesson is about the half the guide is warning you not to lose.

The map is a floor, not a ceiling

“Your evaluation need not be limited to these points… If you want to comment on other aspects of the person's efforts, you are welcome to do so.” And ask the speaker what they are working on: the form's own advice is that your evaluation is most helpful when you know their goals and the areas they want help with. The printed criteria are the default map. The speaker's own goal is an addition to it.

Section 2Two families, and only one of them is visible

Of the seven core criteria, one — Interest — scores the speech's substance. The other six score how the speech was performed. The two groups behave so differently that treating them as one flat list is the most consequential mistake an evaluator can make. This course groups them into the two families:

C
Content — what the speech gives the audience

Its purpose, its structure, and its reason to be worth listening to. Judged by reconstruction: there is no moment at which you watch structure happen. You can only work out afterwards whether it was there.

D
Delivery — how the speaker transmits it

In three channels. Voice: clarity and vocal variety. Body: eye contact, gestures, posture, movement. Connection: audience awareness and comfort level — the two criteria about the speaker's relationship with the room.

The vocabulary is Toastmasters' — its General Speech Purpose rubric even reads “excellent content and exemplary delivery” — but the official forms list the criteria flat. The grouping, and the three-channel split of delivery, are this course's teaching device; the criteria and their definitions are theirs. (The Quick Card's four observation channels are Content, Voice, Body and Effect on you, where the fourth is your own reaction. Same criteria, different question: the card says what to watch while the speech is happening; this lesson says what the speech is made of.)

Why the split matters before you open your mouth. Three mechanisms.

(a) They fail differently, and each one's symptoms point at the other

Delivery failure announces itself during the speech as a sensation: the room feels flat, you feel hurried. Content failure arrives through the same sensations, pointing the wrong way. A speech with no structure feels rushed, because you are trying to hold unconnected things; a speech with no stakes feels flat, because there is nothing in it to lift. Report the sensation and you have handed over a symptom. Report the family and you have made a diagnosis.

(b) They are observed at different speeds

Delivery can be recorded continuously — pitch, pace, eyes, hands — and the record is complete while the speech is still running. Content cannot: purpose and structure are properties of the whole, so they only become checkable once the whole exists, after the speaker stops. That gives an evaluator two jobs rather than one — watch continuously, then reconstruct. Most beginners skip the second, which is why their evaluations end up delivery-only without anyone deciding they should be.

(c) The relationship criteria are symptoms, not skills

Audience Awareness and Comfort Level look like things to work on directly. They are not: both are the audience's read of everything else. A speaker who knows what their speech is for, and where it is going, tends to look comfortable and to read the room; one improvising through an unbuilt speech grips the lectern and addresses the back wall. The behaviour is real and worth reporting — the speaker cannot see it — but report it knowing it may be a symptom rather than a separate fault. Neither family rescues the other, either: delivery cannot make an empty speech matter, and content cannot make an unheard idea land.

Section 3The delivery half, criterion by criterion

These are the criteria you can prove, because you watched them happen. The Excellence Ladder defines every level; what follows is what each one adds and what its absence costs.

Voice

CriterionWhat it addsWhat its absence costs
Clarity
“Spoken language is clear and is easily understood”		 Access — the pipe every other quality flows through. An idea that was not understood was not delivered, however good it was. Attention moves from thinking to decoding, and stops trying once the work costs more than it returns.
Vocal Variety
“Uses tone, speed, and volume as tools”		 Meaning. The words carry the information; the voice carries which of it matters, how the speaker feels about it, and whether a line is a joke or a warning. Item 199: “A one-note speaker is tedious to an audience and promotes inattention and boredom.”6 Everything arrives at the same weight, so the audience must decide what is important — and since the speaker never told them, they choose nothing.

Clarity is the one criterion with two failure points. “I had to work to catch the ends of your words” is articulation; “I lost you halfway through that sentence” is usually one sentence doing the work of three. Same criterion, opposite fixes — so say which one you mean.

Body

CriterionWhat it addsWhat its absence costs
Eye Contact
“Effectively uses eye contact to engage audience”		 Being spoken to rather than spoken at — the one mechanism that makes a one-to-many speech one-to-one. It has a second job: at level 4 it “gauges audience reaction and response”, and at level 5 it “uses eye contact to convey emotion and elicit response”. The room becomes spectators, and the speaker flies blind — unable to see confusion or delight, and so unable to respond to either.
Gestures
“Uses physical gestures effectively”		 A second channel for the same message — size, position, number, emphasis — in four official types: descriptive, emphatic, suggestive and prompting (glossary §C). Everything must pass through words alone; worse, the body can contradict them. Hands pinned to the sides while saying “I was terrified” tells the audience not to believe it.
Movement
project criterion, 8104: “Purposeful movement” and “Unintentional movement”		 Energy and emphasis, and it makes the room part of the speech: stepping out from behind the lectern at the close is an argument. Toastmasters scores both directions — “speech is strengthened by purposeful choices of movement”, while “unintentional movement is limited and rarely noticeable”.4 Unintentional movement gives the eye something to track while the ear is trying to follow — rocking, swaying, weight-shifting. A pure blind spot: the speaker cannot feel it.

Connection

CriterionWhat it addsWhat its absence costs
Audience Awareness
“Demonstrates awareness of audience engagement and needs”		 Adjustment — the difference between delivering a script and giving a speech. It depends on all the others: you cannot respond to something you cannot read. The speech proceeds at whatever level the speaker chose in advance. A joke that dies is repeated harder; a delighted room is not given its extra beat.
Comfort Level
“Appears comfortable with the audience”		 Trust. Audiences take their cue from the speaker's body, and uneasy rooms listen worse. Note the form's verb — appears: this is the audience's inference, not the speaker's state. “I noticed you were gripping the lectern for the first minute” is reportable; “you seemed nervous” is not. Attention moves from the message to the messenger.
This half is the provable half

Everything in Section 3 is something you saw or heard, in a place you can point to — which is exactly why it should not be all you report. An evaluation made only of this section is accurate, safe, and about the wrapper.

Section 4The content half, and why it is the hard half

Six criteria tell you how the speech arrived. One tells you what arrived — and the official rubric quietly admits that this one is two criteria wearing a single name. This is the level 2 definition of Interest:

Content is interesting but not well-constructed or is well-constructed but not interesting.

Toastmasters International, Ice Breaker evaluation form (Item 8101E), Evaluation Criteria2

Two independent axes — and the same rubric supplies the ladder that follows from them:

StateWhat it sounds like, and what actually fixes it
Interesting and well-constructed
level 3 and above		 You follow it easily and want to know what happens next. Both axes are working. This is the target.
Interesting, not well-constructed
level 2		 A great yarn: you remember the story and not the point. The fix is structure — the route from the story to the point. More material will not help; it already has material.
Well-constructed, not interesting
level 2		 A lecture: you can follow every step and you do not want to. The fix is stakes, relevance, specificity. More structure will not help; it already has structure.
Neither
level 1		 Nothing to follow and nothing to want. Content is rebuilt from the purpose up, not repaired. Rare, and worth saying plainly when you see it.

Purpose: the standard of relevance

Every speech has two purposes. The general purpose — to inform, to persuade, to entertain, to inspire — “has a general purpose whether it is defined by the speaker or not.” What it adds is a contract: it tells the audience what they are there to do, and they calibrate how they listen. A sales pitch to an audience expecting information meets resistance.

The specific purpose is the sharper instrument — “a one-sentence statement about what you hope to accomplish… worded from the audience's point of view,” clarifying “exactly what you want the audience to be able to do when you've finished your presentation.”3 What it adds is a standard: every sentence can be tested against it, and the conclusion has somewhere to arrive. Without one, nothing is off-topic, so nothing can be cut — which is why purposeless speeches are almost always overfull.

Here is the failure this section exists to catch, in 8103's own words:

The interesting thing about developing a speech is that you can have a very clear idea of what you want to say without ever considering why you are saying it.

Toastmasters International, Writing a Speech With Purpose (Item 8103), p. 123

The speaker has lived with the material for weeks; the audience hears it once, cold. Certainty about the facts conceals the absence of a point for the listener — and you detect it the way any listener does, by noticing that you cannot say what the speech was for. The test you can run from your seat: in the minute after the speech, before you look at your notes, finish “The speaker wanted me to…” If you cannot, you have found the largest available finding in the speech.

Structure: the substitute for a rewind button

The official shape — opening, body, conclusion, transitions at five specific points — is not the mechanism. The mechanism is this: a listener cannot rewind. Speech arrives one sentence at a time and working memory holds only a handful of items; six minutes at a normal speaking rate is roughly 720–960 words. Structure is what turns those hundreds of words into one idea — the point in one hand, a few markers in the other. Remove it and the audience keeps whatever was most vivid, usually the stories, and loses the argument. Toastmasters states the effect plainly: “when your speech is well-structured and easy to follow, it is more effective.”

What structure is not

Not the outline. It is what the audience can hear: signposts, transitions, a return to the opening, a close that closes. A perfect outline that never reaches the audience's ear is a private plan — which is why “I couldn't follow it” is a legitimate finding even when the speech made sense on paper.

Failure signature: great parts, no whole — you can retell the anecdote but not the argument. And proportion is structural: four minutes on the first point and forty seconds on the other two tells the audience what the speaker believed mattered.

Interest: whether it deserved the time

“Interesting” is not “entertaining”. It means the audience has a reason to give the speech attention — stakes, relevance, specificity (“when you described the fudge cake, my mouth watered”, not “you described dessert”), surprise, story. It is the only criterion that asks whether the speech should have been given at all, and the one a technically accomplished speech can miss entirely: a speaker can be clear, varied, grounded and warm, and have nothing to say.

Why you will miss content faults

It is invisible in real time. There is no moment at which “no structure” happens; the whole does not exist until the speech is over. Delivery gets recorded; content has to be reconstructed.

It arrives disguised as delivery. Boredom becomes “your voice was flat”; confusion becomes “you went too fast”. Each disguise names a delivery tool, so the speaker goes home and practises that tool — two weeks of work on the wrong thing, with your authority behind it.

The form points you at delivery. Six of the seven criteria describe the performance, and the performance is the part you can watch happening. That is precisely the bias Item 202 warns about.

The reconstruction test

Sixty seconds after the speech ends, before you look at your notes: can you finish “The speaker wanted me to…”? Can you name the main points, in order? Can you name one thing you will still remember tomorrow, and why? If the first two fail, your biggest finding is a content finding — write it down first, because it is the one you are least likely to recall when you stand up. This test is this course's term; the rules under it are official.

Section 5Choosing your two or three points

The official instruction is unambiguous about the size of an evaluation:

You won't have time to cover everything. Instead, simply select two or three points which you feel are most important and elaborate on them.

Toastmasters International, Effective Evaluation (Item 202), pp. 4–51

Which raises the question the guide does not answer: most important by what measure? Not by how much they bothered you, and not by how easy they are to see. This course calls the measure the order of repair: when several things are wrong, fix them from the foundation upward, because a fault in one layer surfaces as symptoms in the layer above it — and repairing the symptom does not repair the cause.

1
Purpose — could I tell what the speech was for?

Without a purpose there is no standard against which anything else can be judged — including your own comments.

2
Structure — could I hold it?

A purpose the audience cannot follow in real time was not delivered, however clearly it existed in the speaker's head.

3
Interest — was there anything in it for them?

The layer that decides whether the speech deserved the time; polish adds no reason to care.

4
Delivery — did it reach me?

Genuine, reportable problems, and still downstream of what the speech was and whether it was built.

5
Polish — filler words, a repeated gesture, a nervous habit

The last few per cent of effect, and the first thing a beginner spends all three points on.

Two consequences. Upstream fixes dissolve downstream symptoms: a speaker who is certain what the speech is for, and knows the route, tends to slow down, vary more, stand stiller and look up — without practising any of those things. Uncertainty leaks, as hedging, rushing and apologising. Downstream fixes cannot repair upstream faults: a fortnight of pace drills produces a smoother speech with no point.

The one exception. This is not “content always wins”. If the audience could not hear the speaker, or could not follow the words at all, that outranks content — nothing else reached you, so your content judgement may be an artefact of not having received the speech. When the pipe is broken, report the pipe first.

The rule that keeps delivery in its place

Report a delivery fault when it changed what you received — not merely when you noticed it. If the shaking hands cost you the opening line, it earns a point. If you noticed and it cost you nothing, it belongs on the written form. This is Lesson 01's SO WHAT coordinate, turned on your own attention.

And the slots are not only for problems. A strength named precisely is a behaviour the speaker can repeat on purpose — and since the delivery strengths are the easy ones to spot, the content strengths are the ones worth hunting for.

DrillsSolidify it

Reading this section produces fluency — the feeling that you understand it. The drills are what produce storage strength: the ability to run the map in a real meeting, on a speech you have never heard, with your heart going. Do them in order, and do not skip the recall.

Drill 1 · Retrieval practice Draw the map from memory

  3. The two independent axes inside Interest, and which fix each failure needs.
  4. The order of repair, first to last, and why content comes before delivery.

The effort of failing to recall is the mechanism, not a sign that you learned nothing. Sit with a blank for thirty seconds before you scroll.

Purpose → structure → interest → delivery → polish. Content comes first because a downstream fix cannot repair an upstream fault, while an upstream fix often dissolves the downstream symptom — and because a purposeless speech has no standard against which any other judgement can be made.

Drill 2 · Discrimination Content or delivery?

Twelve observations, exactly as an evaluator might write them in the margin of the form. Six are content findings; six are delivery. You get immediate feedback on each. Aim for 12 out of 12 — and read the explanation even when you are right, because several of these are deliberately disguised.

“I lost the thread when you moved from your second point to your third.”

“Your voice stayed on one pitch for the whole last third.”

“I could not have told you, at the end, what you wanted me to do.”

“I noticed you were looking at the ceiling during the story about your father.”

“The room laughed, and you were three sentences further on before it stopped.”

“Every sentence was clear, and I still could not tell you what the speech was about.”

“You were gripping the lectern for the first minute.”

“You gave me three things about your grandmother and I can only remember the last one.”

“I had to work to catch the ends of your words.”

“The facts were correct, but at no point did the topic have anything to do with me.”

“I saw the same ‘slicing the air’ gesture eleven times.”

“You spent four minutes on the first point and forty seconds on the other two.”

Drill 3 · Production Name it and prove it

Recognising a family is not the skill. Diagnosing is. Below are five sentences an evaluator might actually say — the kind that get nods in the room and help nobody. For each one, write down:

  1. Which family, and which criterion.
  2. What you would have had to observe to say it.
  3. The sentence you would actually say out loud, with the behaviour, the location, and the effect on you.

Then reveal the model answers. Note that two of the five have more than one possible diagnosis — that is the point. The criterion name is what forces you to choose.

Symptom 1

“It was a bit flat.”

Model answer

Delivery — vocal variety, and possibly content — interest. “Flat” is a feeling, not a finding. It has at least two distinct causes and the sentence does not say which one you mean.

What you would need to have observed: where in the speech the flatness started, what changed there, and whether the cause was the voice, the material, or both.

If the cause was the voice: “From your second point to the end, every sentence arrived at the same pitch and the same speed, so I stopped hearing where one idea finished and the next began. I noticed it first in the story about your first job.”

If the cause was the material: “The three facts you gave all carried the same weight, so I had no way to know which one mattered most. Losing two of them, and spending the time on the one you cared about, would have given me something to feel.”

WHAT uniform pitch and pace — or undifferentiated material · WHEN the second point onward, first noticed in the story · SO WHAT “I stopped hearing where one idea ended”

Symptom 2

“I got lost in the middle.”

Model answer

Content — structure. “Lost” is almost always about the route, not the voice. The specific fault is usually a missing transition at one of the five points where Toastmasters says a bridge is needed.

What you would need to have observed: the exact place you lost the thread, and what you were still holding when it happened.

Say: “I was with you through the first point. Then you moved into the second without signalling the move, so I was still carrying the first one and I missed the opening of the second. A single sentence — ‘that's the why; here's the how’ — would have carried me across.”

WHAT a missing transition between point one and point two · WHEN the move into the second point · SO WHAT “I was still carrying the first one and missed the opening of the second”

Symptom 3

“You seemed nervous.”

Model answer

Delivery — comfort level. “Seemed nervous” is an inference about the inside of the speaker's head, which you cannot observe and they cannot act on. The criterion is appears comfortable, so report what produced the appearance.

What you would need to have observed: the behaviours, and when they stopped or started.

Say: “For the first minute your weight was shifting from foot to foot and you had both hands on the lectern. It stopped when you got into the story — and that is when I started hearing your words instead of watching your hands.”

WHAT weight shifting, both hands on the lectern · WHEN the first minute, ending when the story began · SO WHAT “I started hearing your words instead of watching your hands”

Symptom 4

“The ending didn't land.”

Model answer

Content — purpose. “Land” is the feeling of arrival, and arrival is only possible if there was a destination. The conclusion is the climax; a close that does not resolve the purpose cannot land, no matter how confidently it is delivered.

What you would need to have observed: what you were still waiting for when the speaker stopped.

Say: “I was still waiting for the idea to resolve when you said thank you. I could tell you what had happened in the story, but not what you wanted me to take away from it. A last line that answered the question your opening raised would have closed the circle for me.”

WHAT an unresolved close · WHEN the final line, at “thank you” · SO WHAT “I was still waiting for the idea to resolve”

Symptom 5

“It felt rushed.”

Model answer

Two candidates, and they need opposite fixes. Delivery — pace: the words themselves came too fast, and the fix is a voice habit. Content — structure: the amount of material forced the speed, and the fix is an editing pass. Getting this wrong sends the speaker to a pace drill when they needed to cut a point.

What you would need to have observed: whether the sentences were genuinely fast, or whether the number of ideas left no room for any of them.

If the pace was the problem: “In the middle section your sentences came out at roughly twice the speed of your opening, and I had to let go of the detail to keep hold of the story.”

If the material was the problem: “You had four points to make in six minutes, so each one got about ninety seconds. I never finished absorbing one before the next arrived. Two of them in full would have been stronger than four in summary.”

WHAT pace, or material volume · WHEN the middle section · SO WHAT “I had to let go of the detail” / “I never finished absorbing one before the next arrived”

Score each of your five diagnoses

  • Did you name a family, not just a feeling? “Delivery” alone is not enough — name the criterion. The criterion is what tells the speaker which tool to practise.
  • Did you say what you would need to have observed? If the diagnosis does not require any particular evidence, it is a mood, not a finding.
  • For the two multi-cause symptoms (1 and 5), did you notice both possibilities? If you chose one without saying why, you may have sent the speaker to the wrong practice.
  • Does your spoken sentence contain WHAT, WHEN and SO WHAT? A diagnosis without an anchor is a verdict with better vocabulary.
  • Did you stay out of the speaker's head? “You seemed nervous” describes a person; “your hands were on the lectern” describes something they can change.

Two or more checks failed on any item? Rewrite that item now. The repetition is the point.

Drill 4 · Field work Count your own attention

This is where the lesson leaves the page. At your next club meeting — or on any speech you can watch end to end — do not try to evaluate. Just watch, and every time you catch yourself forming an observation, press the button for the family it belongs to.

Do not deliberate. If you are unsure, press whichever one it feels like. The point is not to be right; the point is to find out where your attention goes when nobody is steering it.

Almost everyone returns a delivery-heavy count

Delivery is visible, it happens continuously, and it is what your eye is trained to follow. Content is invisible in real time and only checkable by reconstruction. So a beginner's attention lands on delivery by default — usually by a wide margin. That is not a personal failing; it is the bias the official guide warns about, and the whole reason Section 4 exists.

Two questions to ask of your own count

  • Could you finish “The speaker wanted me to…”? If not, you produced a delivery-only evaluation of a speech whose content you never checked — the exact failure this lesson is about. The purpose sentence you just wrote is the finding you would otherwise have missed.
  • Could you name the main points, in order? If you can, structure was working. If you cannot, and you still rated the speech well, ask what standard you were using.

The corrective exercise

Run the same speech again if you can — a recording, or the next speaker at the next meeting — and force yourself to spend the first two minutes on content only: purpose, structure, and whether you cared. Then check whether your delivery observations were really delivery, or whether several of them were content faults wearing a delivery costume.

Bring the ratio to our next session. A count that is still 90/10 after five speeches tells us something worth working on; a count that has moved toward the middle tells us the map has taken hold.
