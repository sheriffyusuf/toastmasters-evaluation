Lesson 04 · Delivery

Voice: Pitch, Tone, Volume, Pace

The second text — what the voice says that the words cannot, and how to name its faults precisely enough to be useful.

Mission: diagnose voice from observed behaviour, not impression Time: ~20 min reading + ~35 min drills Prerequisite: Lesson 02

Lesson 02 called the delivery half the provable half. Provable is not the same as easy to name. Voice is the criterion every evaluator has an opinion about and almost none can specify: “you were monotone” is a sentence about a whole speech, with no moment in it, and the speaker cannot act on it. This lesson gives you the five tools the voice is made of, the failure signature of each, and the distinctions that decide which advice helps.

Section 1The voice is the second text

Start with the claim that makes voice an evaluator's problem rather than a matter of taste:

Your greatest tool as a speaker is your voice… When you speak, your voice is the primary link between you and your listeners. It is the medium of your message.

Toastmasters International, Your Speaking Voice, p. 3

Read medium strictly. A transcript of a speech is not the speech, and everyone knows it: something was lost that was never in the words. That something is carried by the voice, and it is not decoration. The voice does three jobs the text cannot do, and each can be observed going wrong.

It says which word is the point

Take seven words and change nothing but the pitch. This is Toastmasters' own demonstration:

I was born in Australia. (You, on the other hand, were born somewhere else.)
I was born in Australia. (How dare you imply that I wasn't?)
I was born in Australia. (I'm a native — not a newcomer.)
I was born in Australia. (Not outside Australia.)
I was born in Australia. (Not in New Zealand.)

Toastmasters International, Your Speaking Voice, p. 6

Five sentences, identical in every word, five meanings — and the difference exists only in pitch. That is what monotone destroys. The speech does not merely become less pleasant; information is deleted. When a whole speech arrives on one note, the audience must decide for itself which words mattered, and because the speaker never told them, they choose nothing.

It holds attention, and sets the relationship

Your Speaking Voice is blunt about the alternative to variety: “A one-note speaker is tedious to an audience and promotes inattention and boredom.”Your Speaking Voice

Two scores, five tools

On the Pathways form, voice is scored twice: Clarity — “spoken language is clear and is easily understood” — and Vocal Variety — “uses tone, speed, and volume as tools.”Vocal Variety and Body Language Pitch, tone, volume, pace and pause are not scores; they are the parts you inspect to explain one. Reporting a tool as the verdict is how “you were monotone” becomes a whole-speech sentence with nothing the speaker can locate.

The flat-read test

Take one line the speech depended on and read it back to yourself with the pitch flattened to a single note. Whatever drops out of the meaning was being carried by the voice. On “I was born in Australia”, four of the five meanings vanish — so you have a location: this line, this moment, this much meaning lost. The flat-read test is this course's name for the procedure, not Toastmasters'; it only formalises what the demonstration above already proves.

Section 2Vocal variety: four components and a fifth tool

The official list is short: “vocal variety is made up of pitch, tone, volume, and pace.”Vocal Variety and Body Language Take them one at a time: what each controls, and the signature of its failure.

Pitch

What it is. How high or low the voice sounds. There is no correct pitch for public speaking, and this matters to an evaluator: a speaker whose natural pitch you dislike is a preference, not a fault. What is measurable is range. “It can be helpful to extend your pitch. This is particularly important for individuals with a narrow range who may sound monotone… Accomplished speakers often have a wide range in pitch,” and a good speaker “may use as many as 25 different notes to convey variety and meaning.”Vocal Variety and Body Language · Your Speaking Voice

The unit of pitch is the word. An inflection is a raised pitch — a high note used to add emphasis to a word — and Your Speaking Voice catalogues four movements available inside a single word: rising, falling, rising then falling, and falling then rising.Your Speaking Voice A speaker using only the fifth option, no change, has not lost a stylistic preference; they have lost the tool the sentence depends on.

How it fails. Monotone: the pitch is always the same. Narrow range: it moves, but only between two or three notes, which the audience experiences as flat even though it is not technically monotone. And sitting too high: the whole range is shrill rather than low and full. Your Speaking Voice's poles are exact — the desired speaking pitch is “low, clear, full, and varied”; the failing one is “squeaky, guttural, shrill, or flat.”Your Speaking Voice

Tone

What it is. “Your tone is the emotion behind the sound of your voice. It is the quality that tells an audience how you feel about the words you are saying” — happy, sad, excited, or whatever the material calls for.Vocal Variety and Body Language

The terminology trap

In everyday English, “tone” usually means pitch: “I didn't like his tone of voice” almost always means it was high. Toastmasters separates the two, and so must you. Pitch is where the voice sits; tone is what it feels like. This is not pedantry — the two faults have opposite repairs, and a speaker cannot act on a comment that confuses them.

The same property appears in Your Speaking Voice under a second official name, voice quality — “the essence of your speaking sound. It expresses emotional color” — with the poles listed as full, clear, mellow and enthusiastic at one end, nasal, breathy, harsh and lifeless at the other.Your Speaking Voice The two manuals use different words for one instrument — tone in Vocal Variety and Body Language, voice quality in Your Speaking Voice — and the Speech Profile records them together (Section 5).

Why it is hard to recommend against. Tone is largely involuntary, and Your Speaking Voice locates the cause precisely: “The primary cause of negative voice quality is tension — emotional or physical tension — so controlling tension is critical to improving your voice quality.” Then the mechanism: “Tight muscles combined with inadequate breath support cause disagreeable tones that are thin, nasal, high-pitched, and lacking in resonance.”Your Speaking Voice That pair of sentences is the most useful thing here for an evaluator. It explains why “just relax” has never worked — the speaker already knows — and why a tone fault so often travels with a volume fault and an articulation fault: tight muscles and shallow breath produce all three. What you recommend instead is not control of the sound, but release of the tension producing it.

Volume

Two jobs. Loudness decides whether you are heard: too quiet and “your audience will struggle to hear you”; too loud and “audience members may be too distracted to listen to the content.”Vocal Variety and Body Language The operative words are otherwise consistent. Volume only emphasises anything by contrast.

Four observations that are not one fault. “You were too quiet” usually reports one of these, and they need different recommendations:

  • Inaudible. The voice does not reach the back of the room at all — often a room or placement problem rather than a voice problem.
  • Fading. “A fading voice is first cousin to a whisper; a sentence or phrase may begin well enough, then collapse into unintelligibility.”Your Speaking Voice This one is locatable: the second half of the sentence, or the last item in the list.
  • Soft on purpose. Not a fault. “Inaudibility is different from speaking softly with vocal support. A whisper is air without sound; air must vibrate against your vocal cords to produce audible sound.”Your Speaking Voice A deliberate whisper that lands is a technique; a voice with no breath under it is a fault. Telling the two apart is the difference between a compliment and a criticism.
  • Loud with no contrast. The tool is spent. The reveal and the equipment list arrive at the same loudness, so nothing is emphasised — including the loudness.

Projection is not volume. Projection means sending the voice to the back of the room on supported breath; it is a matter of the air column, not of effort. Your Speaking Voice: “Deep, controlled breathing is necessary for good vocal production. Your voice is supported by a column of air, the depth and steadiness of which determines your vocal quality.”Your Speaking Voice The same mechanism is why posture belongs in a lesson about voice: the glossary's note on posture is that it “helps you breathe well and effectively project your voice.” Which is why “speak up” is one of the least useful comments in the evaluator's vocabulary: told to push harder, a speaker tightens the throat, and tight muscles with shallow breath produce the thin, high-pitched, strained sound Your Speaking Voice described above. Told instead to square the shoulders and let the breath carry the line, they get louder without getting worse.

Pace

What it is. Speed, and separately smoothness. The official failure description is symmetrical: “A speaker whose pace is too fast is difficult to understand. Speakers who tend to speak at a slow or plodding pace often lose audience members' interest.”Vocal Variety and Body Language Your Speaking Voice supplies the number and the reason to vary it: the best rate is 120 to 160 words per minute, and “by varying your speaking rate during a talk, you can also reflect changes in emotions and mood, as well as emphasize critical points.”Your Speaking Voice

The mechanism: pace faults eat the other tools. Too fast, and a speaker “may shorten sounds and fail to vary intensity, volume, and pitch”Your Speaking Voice An evaluator who hears a speaker rush and says “you need to slow down” has found a cause and skipped the diagnosis. The questions are which tool it damaged, and where.

Two axes, not one. The Speech Profile scores timing twice over — speed (slow, plodding to fluent, with high speed at the far end) and smoothness (jerky to smooth; hesitant to deliberate).Your Speaking Voice That split matters, because “too fast” and “jerky” are different faults with opposite repairs: rushing wants pauses inserted, while jerky delivery wants the thoughts rehearsed until each one arrives whole. One caveat: Your Speaking Voice warns that rate “is closely associated with your personality… rate is difficult to change because it relates to how you think and behave.” So recommend variation at named moments rather than a new default speed. “Change your pace entirely” is a personality assignment; “pause before the number at the end” is a behaviour.

Pause

What it is. Your Speaking Voice defines it exactly: “Pauses are periods of silence between words and phrases. Their function is to separate ideas and hold attention.”Your Speaking Voice It is a component of vocal variety in its own right, and the only one that works by subtraction.

Why it is essential. Everything else in this section shapes the sound; the pause creates the room the sound needs. A point lands in the silence after it. Churchill's “This — was their finest hour” works only because the dash is there: without it the sentence is ordinary, and with it the two halves arrive separately and the second one has somewhere to land.

How it fails. Three signatures. No pause at all: the speech is one continuous block, and the audience is never given the beat that would tell it something finished. A pause in the wrong place: mid-clause, where it reads as hesitation rather than emphasis. And the filled pause: “um”, “so”, “you know” occupying the space without providing it. The profile's poles capture the distinction an evaluator needs — hesitant against deliberate. Both are silences, and the audience can hear which one it is from what comes after.

Section 3Clarity: the access criterion

Vocal variety is the second voice score on the form. The first is Clarity — “spoken language is clear and is easily understood” — and it earns its place at the top of the criteria list. Rung 3 is “clear and is easily understood”; rung 2 is “somewhat unclear or challenging to understand”; rung 5 is a speaker “who is always understood.”Vocal Variety and Body Language Clarity is access: every other quality, including variety, is delivered through this pipe. While the audience is decoding, it is not thinking, and once decoding costs more than the sentence returns, attention withdraws. That is why a clarity fault is never a small finding, however small the mistake sounds.

Articulation, and its two halves

Your Speaking Voice gives the instrument one name: articulation, “how distinctly you formulate your words when you speak,” which “includes both how you pronounce individual words and how clearly you create speech sounds.”Your Speaking Voice The halves are worth separating, because they are different faults:

  • Pronunciation — “the formation and utterance of words… the product of correct sounds in the sequence of a word.” Its failures come with examples: “just — jist, asked — axed, length — lenth, going to — gunna.”Your Speaking Voice
  • Enunciation — “the fullness and clarity of speech sounds.”Your Speaking Voice Pronunciation plus enunciation gives articulation.

The three conditions, as a diagnostic sequence

Clear articulation requires three conditions, and the list is a procedure.Your Speaking Voice

  1. The sound must be accurately formed. The physical half — tongue, teeth, palate, lips and jaw. The profile's poles name the usual causes: slushy, lazy lips, tangled tongue, tight jaw.
  2. The sound must be sufficiently supported by the breath. This is the same condition that governs volume, which is why the two faults travel together. One cause, two symptoms: a voice that fades at the ends of sentences and a voice that will not carry both come from insufficient breath support. Find this condition failing and you have found a finding that repairs two complaints.
  3. The sound must be completely finished. The swallowed ending, and the condition behind the most common clarity comment in Toastmasters — the one Lesson 02 predicted: “I had to work to catch the ends of your words.”

Two failure points, and only one belongs here. Lesson 02 flagged that Clarity has two: the word and the sentence. “I had to work to catch the ends of your words” is articulation. “I lost you halfway through that sentence” is usually one sentence doing the work of three, and no amount of enunciation practice will fix it — that is a content fault wearing a delivery costume, and the recommendation is to cut the sentence. Say which one you mean; the two fixes do not overlap.

Section 4Six distinctions that change the fix

Voice feedback is rarely wrong; it is undifferentiated. Each of these six sounds like one comment and is two, and what the speaker does next depends on which one you say.

What you heardThe two faults — and the question that tells them apart
“Every sentence was on the same note.” Monotone (never changes) or narrow range (changes between two or three notes). Monotone is built from nothing; a range is widened.
“I didn't like the sound of the voice.” Pitch sitting shrill, or tone that is harsh, nasal or strained. Range versus tension — so “lower your voice” makes the second worse.
“I couldn't hear her.” Inaudible (placement), fading at the ends of points (breath support), or soft on purpose and carrying fine (working technique).
“It felt rushed.” Fast (too many words per minute — insert pauses) or jerky (the thought arrives in fragments — rehearse the thought).
“He seemed unsure.” Slow (a chosen pace) or hesitant (searching for the next thought). Hesitation shows mid-clause; slowness between finished thoughts.
“The voice kept changing.” Deliberate variety (change tied to meaning) or undirected variation (change pointing nowhere). Ask what each change did to you.

That last row guards against an error in the opposite direction. An evaluator who has just learned about vocal variety tends to reward any movement — and an excitable speaker with no control changes everything constantly without emphasising anything.

Section 5From symptom to cause to finding

The audience hears symptoms. “She sounded nervous” is a symptom, and a symptom is neither a fault nor a finding: it cannot be located, so it cannot be acted on. Getting from one to the other is the whole of this section.

One cause, several symptoms

Voice faults cluster around a small number of causes, and Your Speaking Voice named the biggest: tension, usually with shallow breath. Follow that one cause and it produces most of the catalogue at once. Tight throat muscles and inadequate breath support give a tone that is “thin, nasal, high-pitched.” The same shallow breath gives a voice that fades at the ends of sentences. A tight jaw swallows final consonants. And the nerves that produced the tension usually hurry the pace, which shortens sounds and flattens the pitch. Six complaints, one cause.

Toastmasters makes the same point in the other direction, which is what licenses the recommendation: “Focusing on a single behavior or skill can actually help build others. Presentation skills are linked in many ways. For example, moving in a more controlled way may contribute to regulating your speed so you avoid speaking too fast or slow.”Evaluation and Feedback

Why clustering is not optional

Effective Evaluation and Evaluation and Feedback cap an evaluation at two or three points, because “too much information adds stress and may dissuade the speaker from trying again.”Evaluation and Feedback · Effective Evaluation An evaluation that lists fading, flatness, mumbling, rushing and a nasal tone has broken the rule five times. Those are one finding; name it once and show it twice. The words cluster and symptom–cause–finding are this course's; the mechanisms come from Your Speaking Voice and Evaluation and Feedback.

The instrument: the Speech Profile

Voice evaluations become repeatable rather than impressionistic when you use the tool Toastmasters built for them. The Speech Profile in Your Speaking Voice (pp. 18–19) rates each dimension 1–7 from negative to positive, and the one in Vocal Variety and Body Language (pp. 17–18) does the same on an ineffective-to-effective scale.Vocal Variety and Body Language · Your Speaking Voice Both list the same six dimensions.

DimensionRated lowRated high
Loudness or volume Too quiet, inaudible, flat Good projection, easily heard, vibrant and dynamic
Pitch High, shrill, monotonous Low, full, varied
Voice quality (tone) Nasal, breathy, harsh or raspy, lifeless Open, clear, mellow, enthusiastic
Articulation Slushy, lazy lips, tangled tongue, tight jaw, mumbling, mispronunciation Clear, crisp, controlled, open mouth, projection, correct pronunciation
Timing or rate Jerky, slow and plodding, unvaried, hesitant Smooth, fluent, varied and exciting, deliberate
Vocal variety Emotionless, unfriendly, strained, dull Conveys emotion, genial, natural, vital

Two things about using it. It is a diagnostic, not the form: you score Clarity and Vocal Variety, and you use the profile to find which parts produced those scores and which single cause sits behind them. And it is designed to be completed by somebody else — Your Speaking Voice is explicit that “you cannot objectively analyze your own voice,” which is why this skill is worth something to other speakers.

For this project the speaker also declares the focus — vocal variety, body language, or both — and “you are required to share your choices up front, so the evaluator knows which areas of the evaluation resource to focus on.”Vocal Variety and Body Language That decides where your two or three points go, though a clarity fault that stops the speech being understood is a problem whatever the project. The choice belongs to the speaker: “Meeting your personal goals, even if those goals differ from the goals your evaluator laid out for you, is your prerogative.”Evaluation and Feedback

The five-step voice diagnosis

Run this in the minute after the speech, before you read your own notes.

  1. Observe with the profile. Run the six dimensions against what you heard. Resist forming an overall verdict.
  2. Take the loudest symptom. The one that cost you the most as an audience member — not the one that is easiest to describe.
  3. Name the mechanism. Which tool failed, and why did that produce the effect on you?
  4. Test the cause against a second symptom. If breath support is the cause, a fading voice and swallowed endings should both point at it. If the second symptom contradicts your cause, discard the cause, not the evidence.
  5. Say it as behaviour, location, effect — and aim the recommendation once, at the cause.

Phrase patterns for voice findings

Never saySay instead
You need to speak up. From where I sat I had trouble hearing the second half of each point — the sentences started strong and trailed off. Letting the breath carry the line, rather than pushing harder, would keep me with you to the end.
Be more expressive. “I still hadn't told my father” arrived at exactly the weight of the shopping list that followed it. A beat of silence before it — and less volume, not more — would have made it the moment it should have been.
Slow down. In the last two minutes the words were arriving faster than I could assemble them, and I lost the ends of several sentences. One pause at the turn would have bought you the time back without changing your pace.
You were monotone. I could have written your sentences down and read them back on one note without losing anything — and that is the problem. On “I had no idea what to do next” the pitch never moved, so I heard the words but not what they cost you.
Name the strength by its mechanism

Voice is the easiest thing in a speech to compliment vacuously. “Great vocal variety” cannot be repeated, because it does not say what produced it. “You dropped your volume on ‘and that was the moment I stopped’ — I leaned in” names the tool, the moment and the effect, and that is the sentence a speaker remembers when they write their next one.

DrillsSolidify it

Reading this section produces fluency — the feeling that you understand it. The drills produce storage strength: the ability to hear a voice fault, name the tool, and offer a cause-and-effect finding while the room is watching. Do them in order, and do not skip the recall.

Drill 1 · Retrieval practice Recall the instrument

Close the page. Do not scroll up. From memory, write down:

  1. The four components of vocal variety and the fifth tool. One sentence on what each one controls.
  2. The two criteria the Pathways form actually scores for voice, in their own wording.
  3. The three conditions of clear articulation, and the two halves of articulation.
  4. The optimum speaking rate in words per minute, and one mechanism by which each extreme fails.
  5. The primary cause of negative voice quality, and two other faults it produces.
  6. The six dimensions on the Speech Profile.

The effort of failing to recall is the mechanism, not a sign that you learned nothing. Sit with a blank for thirty seconds before you scroll.

Autosaves on this device.

1. Four components, one fifth tool

Pitch — how high or low the voice sits, and where it moves; measured as range, moved by inflection.
Tone — the emotion behind the sound; Your Speaking Voice calls the same thing voice quality, and its positive poles are full, clear, mellow, enthusiastic.
Volume — whether you are heard, and how loudness is used for emphasis.
Pace — speed, and separately smoothness; optimum 120–160 words per minute.
Pause — deliberate silence, which “separates ideas and holds attention.”

2. The two voice scores

Clarity — “spoken language is clear and is easily understood.” Vocal Variety — “uses tone, speed, and volume as tools.”

3. Articulation

Halves: pronunciation (the right sounds in the right sequence) + enunciation (the fullness and clarity of the sounds).
Three conditions: the sound must be accurately formed; sufficiently supported by the breath; completely finished.

4. Rate

120–160 words per minute. Too fast and the speaker “may shorten sounds and fail to vary intensity, volume, and pitch.” Too slow and listeners daydream, because “people think at a much faster rate than the flow of speech.”

5. The single biggest cause

Tension, usually with inadequate breath support: “The primary cause of negative voice quality is tension.” The same cause produces a fading voice (breath), a swallowed ending (tight jaw), and often a hurried pace.

6. The Speech Profile dimensions

Loudness or volume · Pitch · Voice quality · Articulation · Timing or rate · Vocal variety.

Drill 2 · Discrimination Name the tool, name the fault

Each item below is something an evaluator might actually hear, or say to themselves in the moment. Choose the more precise diagnosis. Several items are deliberately close together, so read the explanation even when you are right — the distinction is the point. Aim for 12 out of 12.

“Every sentence arrived on the same note, from the first word to the last.”

“It moved all evening — but only between two or three notes.”

“He described his daughter's birth the way you would read a bus timetable.”

“From the fifth row I gave up on the second half of every point — the sentences started well and trailed away.”

“The volume was set at eleven from the greeting to the last thank-you.”

“Nine hundred words in five minutes, and I could not hold a single sentence.”

“He never stopped between points — the next one began the instant the last one ended.”

“‘The first three weeks’ came out as ‘the firs' three week'.”

“He said ‘axed’ for ‘asked’, twice.”

“She sounded like she was hunting for the next sentence — long gaps in the middle of clauses, not at the ends.”

“By minute three the voice was raspy and nasal, and it sounded strained.”

“The pitch, the volume and the pace changed constantly — but I could not tell you what any change was for.”

Drill 3 · Production Rewrite the vague voice comment

These five comments are said in every club and help nobody — including the last one, which sounds like a compliment and is just as unactionable. For each, write the version you would actually say: the tool, the moment, the effect on you, and a recommendation aimed at the cause. Then reveal the model answer and score yourself against the checklist.

Comment 1

“You need to speak up.”

Model answer

What is wrong with the comment: it names the fix before the observation, and the fix is wrong for one of the three possible faults. If the voice was fading, pushing harder tightens the throat and makes the sound thinner and higher — exactly the mechanism Your Speaking Voice describes.

If the voice was fading: “Each point started strongly and then trailed off — I lost the last few words of every one, including the number at the end of your second point. That is usually the breath running out rather than the voice being quiet, and standing tall and letting the air carry the line usually fixes it without any extra effort.”

If the voice was simply inaudible: “I could not hear you past the second row. I don't think that is about loudness — it is about where the sound is going. Try projecting to the back wall rather than to the front row, and check the room before you start.”

WHAT the second half of each sentence, lost · WHEN the close of every point — best anchored to the specific number · SO WHAT “I lost the last few words of every one”

Comment 2

“Be more expressive.”

Model answer

What is wrong with the comment: “expressive” is not a behaviour. It also gives the speaker no way to know what was missing or where — and it invites them to add emotion indiscriminately, which is Section 4's undirected variation.

Say: “The line ‘I still hadn't told my father’ arrived at the same weight as the list of things you packed. I noticed because I had been waiting for that moment since the opening. A beat of silence before it, and less volume rather than more, would have made it land the way I think you wanted it to.”

WHAT a key line delivered at default pitch and volume · WHEN “I still hadn't told my father” · SO WHAT “I had been waiting for that moment, and it went past me”

Comment 3

“Slow down.”

Model answer

What is wrong with the comment: it is a default-speed instruction, and Your Speaking Voice warns that rate is tied to personality — “difficult to change because it relates to how you think and behave.” It also does not say what the speed cost. Your Speaking Voice names three costs: shortened sounds, flattened volume, flattened pitch.

If the fault was speed: “In the last two minutes the words were arriving faster than I could assemble them, and I lost the ends of several sentences — the point about the second interview in particular. You don't need a slower voice; one pause at the turn would have given me back the time.”

If the fault was smoothness: “It wasn't fast — it was jerky. Each idea seemed to arrive in two halves, and I was still assembling the first when the second started. The pause is the right tool, but it belongs at the ends of thoughts, not in the middle of them.”

WHAT a fault in speed or in smoothness — you must choose · WHEN the last two minutes / the second half of every idea · SO WHAT “I lost the ends of several sentences” / “I was still assembling the first half”

Comment 4

“You were monotone.”

Model answer

What is wrong with the comment: it is a verdict about the whole speech with no location in it, and it may be the wrong verdict — a narrow range and a true monotone need different work. It also says nothing about what it cost the audience, which is the only part the speaker can feel.

Say: “I could have written your sentences down and read them back on one note without losing anything — and that is the cost. On ‘I had no idea what to do next’ the pitch never moved, so I heard the words but not what they cost you. Pick three lines next time — the opening, the turn, and the close — and decide in advance which word in each one is the point. Let the pitch rise on that word. Three is enough to start.”

WHAT a flat pitch across the speech · WHEN “I had no idea what to do next” · SO WHAT “I heard the words but not what they cost you”

Comment 5

“Great vocal variety.”

Model answer

What is wrong with the comment: it is a compliment, not information. The speaker cannot repeat “great”, and cannot tell which of their choices produced it. A strength named precisely is a behaviour they keep; a vague strength is something they thank you for and forget.

Say: “You dropped your volume almost to a whisper on ‘and that was the moment I stopped’. I actually leaned in — the whole room did. It worked because the line before it was at full volume, so the drop had somewhere to drop from. That is the one to build your next speech around.”

WHAT a deliberate drop in volume on a key line · WHEN “and that was the moment I stopped”, about four minutes in · SO WHAT “I leaned in”

Score each of your five rewrites

  • Did you name the tool? Pitch, tone, volume, pace or pause. “Voice” is not a tool, and “delivery” is not one either.
  • Did you pick the right one of the pair? Monotone or narrow range; pitch or tone; quiet or fading or deliberate; fast or jerky; slow or hesitant. Section 4 is the list.
  • Does your sentence contain WHAT, WHEN and SO WHAT? Voice findings are the easiest to leave unanchored, because the fault seems to be everywhere at once. Find one moment anyway — quote the line.
  • Did you reach for the cause? A recommendation aimed at the symptom (“speak louder”, “sound happier”) usually makes it worse. Aim at breath, tension, or placement instead.
  • Did you test your cause against a second symptom? If the cause is breath support, a fading voice and swallowed endings should agree. There is a worked example of the test in Comment 3.
  • Did you keep it to one point per comment? Six observations is not thoroughness; it is a broken rule. Cluster them into the finding that explains the most, and show it twice.

Two or more checks failed on any item? Rewrite that item now. The repetition is the point.

Drill 4 · Field work Record yourself and run the profile

Voice is the one criterion you cannot evaluate from the inside — Your Speaking Voice is explicit that “you cannot objectively analyze your own voice,” which is exactly the reason an evaluator's report is worth having. So this drill puts you on both sides of the instrument. Record two minutes of yourself speaking — a real speech, or read one aloud if you have nothing prepared — and listen back once, without stopping, pressing one button as you go.

Press Varied every time the voice changes one of its tools in a way that matched the meaning: a pitch rise on the word that mattered, a drop in volume on a key line, a pause before a turn. Press Flat every time a line that needed emphasis arrived at the default weight. Do not deliberate; press whichever it felt like.

0 Varied
0 Flat

Then listen a second time — this time with the Speech Profile open in front of you, and a pen. Rate all six dimensions on what you hear, not on what you intended. Finally, answer the prompts below. This is the raw material of an evaluation you could deliver.

Prompt 1

The two dimensions that scored lowest — and the exact moment each one showed itself, with a timestamp.

Strong answer

A timestamp and a description, not a rating alone. “Articulation, 2 out of 7” is a score; “articulation, 1:10 to 1:40 — I swallowed every word ending in t” is a finding. If the moment will not come, the finding may not be real; check it against a second listen before you keep it.

Prompt 2

The single cause that would explain both — and why it fits the second one.

Strong answer

Most people find one of three causes on a first recording. Insufficient breath support — explains fading, swallowed endings, and a voice that will not carry. Tension — explains a thin or nasal tone, a tight jaw, and often a hurried pace. Unprepared thoughts — explains hesitation mid-clause, jerky delivery, and filler words. The test is Prompt 2's real work: if your cause does not explain the second symptom, discard the cause.

Prompt 3

Write the finding you would deliver — behaviour, location, effect — with the recommendation aimed at the cause.

Strong answer

It should contain a quoted line, a clear effect on the listener, and one recommendation. For example: “Each point started strongly and trailed off — I lost the last few words of three of them. That is usually the breath running out rather than the voice being quiet, and standing tall and letting the air carry the line usually fixes it without extra effort.” Note what it does not contain: a verdict about the person, a list of symptoms, or an instruction to try harder.

Prompt 4

The one dimension that scored well — named by its mechanism, so you can keep doing it on purpose.

Strong answer

“At 0:45 I paused for two full seconds before the name of the hospital, and the pause made the next sentence land — that was the only moment in the recording where I could hear myself thinking.” Name the tool, the location and the effect. That version is repeatable; “I was expressive” is not.

Prompt 5

What would a listener have to observe next time to know the fix worked?

Strong answer

Something a stranger could check. “I would hear the final consonant of every word and the last word of every sentence” is observable. “I would feel more confident” is not — and it is also not what the speaker controls. This is the prompt that turns the drill into a plan.

Reading the tally

Most people undercount Varied on the first pass, because they are listening to their own words rather than to their own voice. A high Flat count does not necessarily mean you were monotonous: it means many lines that needed emphasis arrived at the default weight, which is a distribution problem, not a range problem. Look at where the Flat presses cluster. If they cluster at your main points, the fault is emphasis, not variety.

If the profile scores high everywhere

Be suspicious. Self-assessment on a first recording is usually generous, which is precisely Your Speaking Voice's point about objectivity. Ask someone else to rate it — that is the instruction the profile was written with — and compare. The gap between your rating and theirs is the most useful number in this drill.

If two dimensions scored badly and one cause explains both

You have done the thing this lesson is for. That is one finding, not two, and it is ready to be delivered. Note that you have also just proved the mechanism to yourself: the reason the recommendation can be single is that the cause is single.

If the cause will not hold

Do not force the cluster. If breath support does not explain your second symptom, you have two findings, and the two-or-three-point rule leaves room for both. The cluster is a discipline, not a law — and inventing a cause to make a tidier evaluation is exactly the kind of confident guessing this course is trying to replace.

The corrective exercise

Take Prompt 3 and deliver it out loud, to camera, in under forty seconds. Then listen to your own evaluation and run the profile on that. The instrument measures evaluations as well as speeches — and an evaluator whose own voice fades at the ends of sentences has just discovered their next project.
