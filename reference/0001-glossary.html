Reference 01 · Canonical vocabulary

Glossary of Evaluation Terms

The exact words this course uses, defined once. Later lessons assume these definitions and do not redefine them.

Status: canonical — extended, never contradicted Sources: Items 202, 8100, 8104, 199, 1179

Terminology is not decoration. Most bad evaluations are bad because the evaluator reached for an approximate word — “you had no energy” instead of “your pace was uniform” — and the approximation hid both the diagnosis and the fix. Precise words make precise observations possible.

AEvaluation and feedback

Evaluation The feedback a Toastmaster gives on another member's speech, leadership role or project. In Toastmasters, “feedback is called evaluation.”1 It has a written component (the completed form) and a verbal component (a short speech delivered at the meeting).
Evaluator The member assigned to observe a specific speaker and deliver an evaluation. Explicitly not a judge or an authority on speaking — a reporter of their own reaction.1
Feedback Information about what the evaluator observed. Answers the question “how am I being perceived?”2
Example: “I noticed you were shaking.”
Advice An opinion about what the speaker should do.2 Not forbidden, but it belongs after the observation, framed as a possibility, and it should never replace the observation.
Example: “Don't be so scared.”
“I” message A sentence that reports the evaluator's own observation, reaction or opinion using “I”, keeping the focus on behaviour rather than the person. “You were inspiring” is about the person; “I observed how passionately you inspired the audience with your personal story” is about the behaviour.2
Anchor
course term		 A location in the speech attached to an observation — a section, a story, a moment, a minute, a quoted line. Anchoring moves a comment from the task-motivation level to the task-learning level, where feedback actually works. Because the anchor test is a property of the sentence rather than of the speech, you can check it in one second: could the speaker rewind to that exact moment and watch it?
Not official Toastmasters vocabulary. The rule it operationalises — “be specific” — is official; see the evidence base.
The three coordinates
course term		 WHAT (the behaviour — observable, not a trait) · WHEN (the location — the coordinate people omit) · SO WHAT (the effect it had on the evaluator as a listener). A sentence missing WHEN is an impression; a sentence missing SO WHAT is an inspection report.
Content What the speech gives the audience: its purpose, its structure, and its reason to be worth listening to (interest). Judged by reconstruction — purpose and structure are properties of the whole speech, so they only become checkable once it has ended. Scored directly by the Interest criterion (“engages audience with interesting, well-constructed content”) and by the purpose criteria on speechwriting projects.2 7
Delivery How the speaker transmits the speech, in three channels: voice (clarity, vocal variety), body (eye contact, gestures, posture, movement), and connection (audience awareness, comfort level — the two criteria about the speaker's relationship with the room). Judged continuously, as it happens. Content and delivery are the two families; the three-channel split is this course's grouping, the criteria themselves are official.4
Reconstruction test
course term		 Three questions asked in the minute after a speech ends, before consulting notes: can you finish “the speaker wanted me to…”? can you name the main points in order? can you name one thing you will still remember tomorrow, and why? If the first two fail, the largest available finding is a content finding. The test is this course's; the rules under it are official — a specific purpose is stated from the audience's point of view, and the speech must be built to deliver it.7
Shape test
course term		 The evaluator's structure test: after hearing a speech once, can you name the organisational structure it used? If the only description you can produce is a list of topics in order — “she talked about her job, then her mother, then some advice” — then no shape reached the audience, even if the speaker's outline was perfect. The test is this course's; the structures it names, and the rule that a listener cannot rewind, are official.7
Order of repair
course term		 The order in which to fix faults when several exist: purpose → structure → interest → delivery → polish. Upstream faults surface as symptoms downstream, and a downstream fix cannot repair them; an upstream fix often dissolves them. The exception is a delivery failure that prevented the audience from hearing or following the speech at all, which outranks content. Course framing; the underlying instruction — select the two or three most important points rather than the most noticeable — is official.1
Constructive criticism “The practice of pointing out areas that require improvement in a respectful way.”2
Commend – Recommend – Commend (CRC) The three-move shape of an evaluation: open with what worked, place the recommendation in the middle, close with the effect the speech had. Known colloquially as the “evaluation sandwich.” Its purpose is sequencing, not softening.3
Recommendation A specific, practical, positive suggestion for improvement, offered so the speaker can act on it in their next presentation. Distinct from an observation: an observation without a recommendation is a complaint.1
General evaluator The member who evaluates everything in the meeting that has not already been evaluated — the meeting as a whole, the functionaries, and the evaluators themselves.
Speech evaluator The member assigned to one specific prepared speech. This course is about this role.

BVoice

Toastmasters uses four primary components of vocal variety — pitch, tone, volume and pace — plus a fifth tool that rides on top of all of them, the pause.4

Vocal variety
also: modulation		 “The intentional shifting of your voice to create meaning, emotion, and audience engagement.”5 Not decoration — the mechanism by which words carry feeling. Composed of pitch, tone, volume and pace.4
Pitch How high or low the voice sounds. There is no correct pitch for public speaking, but a wide range is the goal. The desired speaking pitch sounds “low, clear, full, and varied.”6
Monotone The pitch of the voice is always the same. A “one-note speaker is tedious to an audience and promotes inattention and boredom.”6
Tone The emotion behind the sound — the quality that tells an audience how you feel about the words you are saying. Tone tells the audience when you are happy, sad or excited.4
Volume Loudness. Does two jobs at once: it determines whether you can be heard, and it is a tool of emphasis. “Shouting in the middle of a speech with an otherwise consistent volume can have an impact — a whisper may be even more powerful.”4
Pace
also: rate		 Speed of speech. The optimum is 120–160 words per minute. Too fast and the speaker “may shorten sounds and fail to vary intensity, volume, and pitch”; too slow and “listeners daydream.”6 Pace should also change during a speech to reflect emotion.6
Pause Silence used deliberately — to build tension, add impact, or let a point land. A component of vocal variety in its own right, distinct from merely stopping.4
Inflection A raised pitch — a high note used to emphasise a word. A single change of inflection can change the meaning of an entire sentence. The classic demonstration: I was born in Australia / I was born in Australia / I was born in Australia / I was born in Australia.6
Projection Sending the voice to the back of the room using supported breath, as distinct from simply being loud. Requires the air to vibrate against the vocal cords — “a whisper is air without sound.”6
Articulation How distinctly words are formed — the shaping of sounds by tongue, teeth, palate, lips and nose. Requires three conditions: the sound is accurately formed, sufficiently supported by breath, and completely finished.6
Pronunciation The formation and utterance of words — correct sounds in the correct sequence. Mispronunciation is the failure to produce the correct sounds (just → “jist”, asked → “axed”).6
Enunciation The fullness and clarity of speech sounds. Pronounce + enunciate = articulate.6
Voice quality The emotional colour of the speaking sound — “the essence of your speaking sound.” Positive: full, clear, mellow, enthusiastic. Negative: nasal, breathy, harsh, lifeless. Caused largely by tension, which is why relaxing the throat is the primary remedy.6
Phonation The production of sound by air pushing the vocal cords apart and their elasticity pulling them back together.6
Resonation The reinforcing and amplifying of sound by the throat, mouth and nasal passages. Explains why voices differ in quality despite identical mechanics.6

CBody language

“The nonverbal expression of emotions, feelings, and ideas.”4 Its components are posture, stance, position, movement, gestures, eye contact and facial expression. A critical caveat: acceptable gestures vary widely between cultures — an innocuous gesture in one culture can be inflammatory in another.4

Posture How you carry yourself. Proper posture is functional, not just aesthetic: it “helps you breathe well and effectively project your voice.” Head high with chin up slightly, back straight, shoulders squared, chest out, stomach in.4
Stance How the body is positioned while standing. Practise with knees relaxed, feet shoulder-width apart, shoulders squared.4
Position Where you are in relation to the audience and the room. The best position is “what feels natural and comfortable” while still communicating the intended message.4
Movement Changing location. “The most visible kind of physical action you can perform” — for example stepping out from behind the lectern. Adds energy and variety.4
Purposeful movement Movement that strengthens the speech because it was chosen. A scored criterion: level 3 = “speech is strengthened by purposeful choices of movement.”4
Unintentional movement Movement the speaker did not choose — swaying, rocking, pacing, fidgeting, shifting weight. Also a scored criterion, and one evaluators frequently miss because it is invisible to the speaker: level 3 = “unintentional movement is limited and rarely noticeable.”4

The four types of effective gesture

Descriptive Indicates position, location, or anything tactical — using the hands to specify a number of objects.4
Emphatic
also: emblematic		 Underscores strong emotion such as earnestness and conviction. Culturally specific, though some are universal (nodding, shrugging). Example: a hand on the heart to communicate powerful belief.4
Suggestive
also: representational		 Gives information about what the speaker is doing — chopping, throwing, picking something up. Symbols of thoughts and emotions: an open palm can suggest giving or receiving ideas.4
Prompting
also: pantomime		 Demonstrates an action the speaker wants the audience to take. Raising your own hand to invite a show of hands.4

Eye contact and facial expression are scored criteria in their own right. Eye contact at level 3 “effectively uses eye contact to engage audience”; at level 5 it is used “to convey emotion and elicit response.”4

DSpeech content and structure

General purpose One of four: to inform, to persuade, to entertain, to inspire. A speech is limited to a single general purpose.7
Specific purpose What the speech is meant to achieve, stated as a single sentence written from the audience's point of view and attainable within the time. Example: “The audience will be able to recognise non-threatening cumulus clouds and identify the difference between those and dangerous cumulonimbus clouds.”7
Thesis
also: main idea		 The one-sentence summary encapsulating the larger message. Everything else in the speech supports it.8
Opening Catches attention, sparks interest in the topic, and leads into the subject. Common failures: apologetic statements, general or obvious observations, long tedious statements, and trite questions such as “Did you ever stop to think…?”9
Body Contains the factual support for the purpose — statements of fact, statements supporting the main idea, and rebuttals to contrary views.9
Conclusion “The climax; the destination you were reaching for when you started.” Should connect back to the opening and finish with confidence. An “inconclusive or apologetic closing can undermine even the best speech.”9
Transition A bridge connecting one thought to the next. Needed at five specific points: from introduction to body, from a main point to a sub-point, from a sub-point to support material, from support material to the next main point, and from final support material to the conclusion.9
Speech value Whether the speech was worth listening to — whether the ideas, logic and purpose had substance. Distinct from delivery: a beautifully delivered empty speech still fails.

The eight organisational structures

An evaluator needs these names because “your structure didn't serve your purpose” is unactionable, whereas “you used a comparative structure but the comparison arrived in the last minute” is not.7

ChronologicalA sequence of events in time order.
TopicalBy topics and subtopics — major concepts, then smaller and smaller subtopics beneath them.
SpatialBy geography or physical structure — moving across a region or landscape.
CausalLinking cause to effect, or effect to its cause.
ComparativeTwo or more objects and their shared and differing attributes.
Problem/SolutionState the issue and its significance, then propose a feasible, advantageous solution.
Particular/General/ParticularA specific example frames broader content; the opening example is echoed in the conclusion to clarify the main message.
Informative / Persuasive / Entertaining / InspiringPurpose-driven shapes that constrain which of the above will work.

EThe rubric and contest scoring

The five levels Pathways scores every criterion on a 1–5 scale: 5 Exemplary · 4 Excels · 3 Accomplished · 2 Emerging · 1 Developing.10 Level 3 is the definition of competent; level 5 is the definition of excellent.
Analytical Quality Contest criterion, 40 points. “The effectiveness of the evaluation.” Were the comments clear and logical? Did the evaluator identify specific strengths and weaknesses? Band labels: Clear, Focused.11
Recommendations Contest criterion, 30 points. Practical, helpful and positive, and they must “enable the speaker to improve his or her next presentation.” Band labels: Positive, Specific, Helpful.11
Technique Contest criterion, 15 points. The manner of delivery — “sensitive to the feelings and needs of the speaker, yet inspire and encourage.” Band labels: Sympathetic, Sensitive, Motivational.11
Summation Contest criterion, 15 points. How the evaluation concludes: briefly summarise, and be positive and encouraging. Band labels: Concise, Encouraging.11
Speech Profile A specialised evaluation resource used when a speaker is being evaluated only on vocal variety. Rates characteristics on an ineffective↔effective scale — loudness, pitch, voice quality, articulation, timing/rate, vocal variety.4
How to use this document

Do not memorise it. Use it as a lookup while you do the drills in each lesson, and let repetition do the work. The terms you will need fastest, under pressure, in a live meeting, are the voice and body language ones — because those are the ones you must name out loud while the speaker is watching you.

FThe evidence base

Toastmasters states the rules; this section is where the mechanisms live. These terms come from the feedback research literature and are used throughout the course to explain why a given piece of Toastmasters guidance is correct.

Feedback Intervention Theory (FIT) Kluger & DeNisi's (1996) theory of how feedback takes effect. Its central claim: feedback changes where the listener's attention goes, and its effectiveness depends on which level of attention it reaches.13
Locus of attention Where a comment sends the listener's attention. The organising variable of the whole course: “FI effectiveness decreases as attention moves up the hierarchy closer to the self and away from the task.”13
Task-learning level The lowest level of the attention hierarchy: attention on the task details. Where feedback works best. Reachable only by naming a specific behaviour at a specific moment.
Task-motivation level The middle level: attention on the focal task in general. “I noticed you were shaking” lives here — about the performance, but not about any particular part of it. Weaker than task-learning.
Meta-task level The highest level, including the self. “You were nervous”, “you're a natural”, normative comparisons. Attention has left the speech; feedback here can reduce performance.
Feedback specificity “The level of information presented in feedback messages.” Specific or elaborated feedback “provides information about particular responses or behaviors beyond just their accuracy.”14
Verification & elaboration Kulhavy & Stock's finding that effective feedback supplies two distinct things: verification (a judgement of whether something was correct) and elaboration (cues that guide the learner). The anchor is what lets a speaker perform their own verification.14
Effect size (d) A standardised measure of how large a difference an intervention makes. The feedback meta-analyses report mean effects around d = 0.41–0.48, alongside enormous variance — roughly a third of feedback interventions make performance worse.13 15
Formative feedback Information communicated to a learner intended to modify their thinking or behaviour for the purpose of improving learning. Distinguished from summative feedback, which judges. A Toastmasters evaluation is formative — which is why “an evaluation is an opinion” and not a verdict.14
An honesty note about the numbers

Toastmasters' own guidance asserts that “positive feedback leads to increased confidence and a greater likelihood of achieving goals”, without citation. The research broadly supports that positive framing helps — but it supports something more uncomfortable too: that roughly a third of feedback hurts, regardless of intent. That is the more important finding, because it means good intentions are not sufficient and technique is not optional.
