Lesson 02 · Foundations

The Anatomy of a Speech

The map of everything you are judging — content and delivery — and why the split decides what you say first.

Mission: name every criterion, and what each one adds Time: ~25 min reading + ~30 min drills Prerequisite: Lesson 01

An audience member listens for meaning. You are listening for parts. That single difference is the job — and it means the first thing an evaluator needs is not an opinion. It is a map: a fixed list of what there is to see, so that what you report is what you looked for rather than what happened to catch your eye.

Section 1The form is already a map

Toastmasters does not hand you a blank page and ask for your feelings about a speech. It hands you a form with a defined list of criteria on it, and one sentence explaining what the form is for:

Read the evaluation guide for the project. The guide explains what you should be looking for as you evaluate. It lists specific questions about the speaker or leader and provides a space in which you may write comments… You will also use this guide as a basis for your verbal evaluation.

Toastmasters International, Effective Evaluation (Item 202), p. 41

You are not being asked to have taste. You are being asked to inspect a defined set of things and report what you found. Every Level 1 speech project uses the same seven core criteria, and they are the spine of the whole discipline:2

CriterionThe form's own wording
ClaritySpoken language is clear and is easily understood
Vocal VarietyUses tone, speed, and volume as tools
Eye ContactEffectively uses eye contact to engage audience
GesturesUses physical gestures effectively
Audience AwarenessDemonstrates awareness of audience engagement and needs
Comfort LevelAppears comfortable with the audience

Projects that focus on a particular skill add criteria on top of those seven: Specific Speech Purpose and General Speech Purpose for Writing a Speech With Purpose; Purposeful Movement and Unintentional Movement for Introduction to Vocal Variety and Body Language; Applied Feedback for the second speech in Evaluation and Feedback. Each criterion is scored 1–5, and every level is defined in Toastmasters' own words — all of them are transcribed in The Excellence Ladder.

So the map is printed for you. What it buys you is not information — it is attention. Four things change the moment you carry it.

1. You see what you look for

A six-minute speech contains far more events than any listener can register: hundreds of sentences, dozens of gestures, countless small decisions. What you notice will never be a sample of all of it. It will be the subset you were watching for. Without a list, that subset is whatever was loudest — a gesture you liked, a filler word that grated, the moment you personally got lost. With the list, you know how many channels exist, so you can place your attention deliberately and you can tell what you failed to check.

2. You stop confusing “noticeable” with “important”

A gesture repeated eleven times is easy to see and small in effect. A speech with no purpose is very hard to notice and enormous in effect. The map does not tell you which is which — the rest of this lesson does — but it tells you that both exist, which is the precondition for comparing them at all. An evaluator who never noticed the second one has not judged the speech; they have judged its surface.

3. You get a shared language

Precise names are not decoration. “Your vocal variety was flat” points at a tool the speaker can practise tomorrow; “you had no energy” points at a mood. The form's names matter twice over, because they are also the names inside the speaker's own project — so your feedback arrives in the vocabulary they are already being trained in, rather than alongside it.

4. You get an official warning about the obvious half

The same page that hands you the map warns you how the map goes wrong:

Good eye contact, meaningful, natural gestures, and correct grammar contribute to the overall effect of a speech but should not be given so much emphasis that they detract from the basic purpose of the evaluation.

Toastmasters International, Effective Evaluation (Item 202), p. 41

Read that as a statement about proportion, not about eye contact. The mechanical criteria are the ones you can watch happening, so they are the ones that will fill your notes. The guide is telling you in advance that this will happen, and that it is a failure. Most of this lesson is about the half it is warning you not to lose.

The map is a floor, not a ceiling

Two more instructions come with the form. First: “Your evaluation need not be limited to these points… If you want to comment on other aspects of the person's efforts, you are welcome to do so.” Second, and it should be automatic — ask the speaker what they are working on. “Your evaluation will be most helpful if you are aware of the person's general goals and of specific areas in which the person would like help and feedback.” If a speaker tells you she is working on her organisation, the form may not mention organisation at all; you should. The printed criteria are the default map. The speaker's own goal is an addition to it, and it outranks anything you brought with you.

Section 2Two families, and only one of them is visible

Here is the observation the rest of this course is built on. Of the seven core criteria, one — Interest — scores the speech's substance. The other six score how the speech was performed. The two groups behave so differently that treating them as one flat list is the most consequential mistake an evaluator can make.

This course groups them into the two families:

C
Content — what the speech gives the audience

Its purpose, its structure, and its reason to be worth listening to. Content is judged by reconstruction: there is no moment at which you watch structure happen. You can only work out afterwards whether it was there.

D
Delivery — how the speaker transmits it

In three channels. Voice: clarity and vocal variety — pitch, tone, volume, pace, pause. Body: eye contact, gestures, posture, movement. Connection: audience awareness and comfort level, the two criteria that describe the speaker's relationship with the room.

The vocabulary is Toastmasters' own — the General Speech Purpose rubric even reads “excellent content and exemplary delivery” — but the official forms present the criteria as a flat list. The grouping into two families is this course's teaching device, and so is the three-channel split of delivery. The criteria themselves, and every definition of what they mean, are theirs.

If you have read the Quick Card

The Evaluator's Quick Card lists four observation channels: Content, Voice, Body, and Effect on you. The fourth is your own reaction, which is Lesson 01's territory. Here the fourth family is Connection — the speaker's relationship with the room. Both lists contain exactly the same criteria. They are answering different questions: the card tells you what to watch while the speech is happening; this lesson tells you what the speech is made of.

Why the split matters before you open your mouth. Four mechanisms.

(a) They fail differently, and each one's symptoms point at the other

Delivery failure announces itself during the speech as a sensation: the room feels flat, you feel hurried, you cannot stop noticing the speaker's hands. Content failure announces itself too — through the same sensations. A speech with no structure feels rushed, because you are trying to hold unconnected things. A speech with no stakes feels flat, because there is nothing in it to lift. A speech with no purpose feels vague, and vagueness is not a property of a voice.

So the sensations are shared, and they are misleading. Report the sensation and you have handed over a symptom. Report the family and you have made a diagnosis. The difference is what the speaker practises for the next fortnight.

(b) They are observed at different speeds

Delivery can be recorded continuously. You can log it as it happens — pitch, pace, eyes, hands — and the record is complete while the speech is still running. Content cannot be recorded that way. Content is a property of the whole speech, so it only becomes checkable once the whole exists, which is to say after the speaker has stopped.

That gives an evaluator two jobs rather than one: watch continuously, then reconstruct. Most beginners do the first and skip the second, which is why their evaluations end up delivery-only without anyone having decided that they should be.

(c) Neither can substitute for the other

Delivery cannot rescue content: a flawless performance of a speech that had nothing in it produces a pleasant six minutes and leaves no memory. Content cannot rescue delivery: a penetrating idea that the audience cannot hear or follow stays a private thought. Both failures are total. They simply fail on opposite sides of the room.

(d) The two relationship criteria are symptoms, not skills

Audience Awareness and Comfort Level sit on the form like skills, so they look like things to work on directly — “be more comfortable”, “be more aware of the room”. They are not. Both are the audience's read of everything else. A speaker who knows exactly what their speech is for, and where it is going, tends to look comfortable and to read the room. A speaker improvising their way through an unbuilt speech tends to grip the lectern and address the back wall. The behaviour you see is real, and it is worth reporting, because the speaker cannot see it. But when you report it, know that you may be reporting a symptom — and say so rather than implying the speaker should simply relax.

Section 3The delivery half, criterion by criterion

The Excellence Ladder defines every level of every criterion. This section answers the question the form does not: what does each one add to a speech, and what is lost when it is missing?

Voice

CriterionWhat it addsWhat its absence costs
Clarity
“Spoken language is clear and is easily understood”		 Access. Clarity is the pipe every other quality flows through — an idea that was not understood was not delivered, however good it was. It has two failure points, and they live in different families: a sentence can be built too densely to follow (a content choice) or spoken so carelessly that its ends are swallowed (a delivery choice). The audience's attention moves from thinking to decoding. Listeners who have to work at the words spend their effort there, and they stop trying once it keeps costing more than it returns.
Vocal Variety
“Uses tone, speed, and volume as tools”		 Meaning. The words carry the information; the voice carries which of that information matters, how the speaker feels about it, and whether a line is a joke, a warning or a confession. Item 199 puts the stakes in one line: “A one-note speaker is tedious to an audience and promotes inattention and boredom.”6 Everything arrives at the same weight, so the audience must decide what is important — and since the speaker never told them, they choose nothing. A monotone speech also feels longer than it is, because the listener has no landmarks to hold.

Clarity deserves a note, because it is the criterion most often mis-diagnosed. “I had to work to catch the ends of your words” is articulation — a voice problem. “I lost you halfway through that sentence” is usually language — one sentence doing the work of three, which is an editing problem. Same criterion, opposite fixes. When you comment on clarity, say which of the two you mean, or the speaker will practise the wrong one.

Body

CriterionWhat it addsWhat its absence costs
Eye Contact
“Effectively uses eye contact to engage audience”		 Being spoken to, rather than being spoken at. A speech is one-to-many; eye contact is the only mechanism that makes it one-to-one, for a second at a time, with each person in the room. The form's levels reveal a second job: at level 4 it “gauges audience reaction and response” — it is the speaker's instrument for reading the room — and at level 5 it “uses eye contact to convey emotion and elicit response”. The room becomes an audience in the passive sense: spectators. Worse, the speaker is flying blind. They cannot see confusion, boredom or delight, so they cannot respond to any of it.
Gestures
“Uses physical gestures effectively”		 A second channel for the same message. Gestures say quickly what words say slowly: size, position, number, and emphasis. Toastmasters names four working types — descriptive, emphatic, suggestive and prompting (glossary §C). Everything must pass through the narrow channel of words. Worse, the body can contradict them: hands pinned to the sides while saying “I was terrified” quietly tells the audience not to believe the sentence.
Movement
project criterion, 8104: “Purposeful movement” and “Unintentional movement”		 Energy and emphasis — and it makes the room part of the speech. Stepping out from behind the lectern at the close is an argument about what is coming. Toastmasters scores both directions: “speech is strengthened by purposeful choices of movement”, while “unintentional movement is limited and rarely noticeable”.4 Unintentional movement gives the eye something to track while the ear is trying to follow — rocking, swaying, shifting weight, pacing. It is a pure blind spot: the speaker cannot feel it, which is exactly why only an observer can report it.

Connection

CriterionWhat it addsWhat its absence costs
Audience Awareness
“Demonstrates awareness of audience engagement and needs”		 Adjustment — the difference between delivering a script and giving a speech. Watch the room, notice what it is doing, respond to it. It is the only criterion that depends on all the others: you cannot respond to something you cannot read. The speech proceeds at whatever level the speaker chose in advance. A joke that dies is repeated harder. A point that confused people is passed over. A delighted room is not given the extra beat it was offering.
Comfort Level
“Appears comfortable with the audience”		 Trust. Audiences take their cue from the speaker's body: a speaker who looks like they want to be somewhere else makes the room uneasy, and uneasy rooms listen worse. Note the form's verb — appears. This criterion is the audience's inference, not the speaker's inner state, which is why “I noticed you were gripping the lectern for the first minute” is the reportable version and “you seemed nervous” is not. Attention moves from the message to the messenger. The audience spends its time wondering what is wrong instead of following what is being said.
This half is the provable half

Everything in Section 3 is something you observed with your own eyes or ears, in a place you can point to. These are the findings that cannot be argued with, which makes them easy and satisfying to report — and it is precisely why they should not be all you report. An evaluation made only of Section 3 is accurate, safe, and about the wrapper.

Section 4The content half, and why it is the hard half

Six criteria tell you how the speech arrived. One tells you what arrived — and the official rubric quietly admits that this one is two criteria wearing a single name. Here is the level 2 definition of Interest, in Toastmasters' own words:

Content is interesting but not well-constructed or is well-constructed but not interesting.

Toastmasters International, Ice Breaker evaluation form (Item 8101E), Evaluation Criteria2

That is not a vague complaint. It is a description of two independent axes, and it says a speech can fail on exactly one of them. The same rubric then supplies the ladder that follows from it:

StateWhat it sounds like, and what actually fixes it
Interesting and well-constructed
level 3 and above		 You follow it easily and want to know what happens next. Both axes are working — the speech has something in it, and the something is built. This is the target.
Interesting, not well-constructed
level 2		 A great yarn. You remember the story and not the point. The temptation is to praise it and move on; the useful comment names the missing part — structure, the route from the story to the point. More material will not help. It already has material.
Well-constructed, not interesting
level 2		 A lecture. You can follow every step and you do not want to. The temptation is to say “good structure” and stop; the useful comment is about stakes, relevance and specificity — a reason for this audience to care. More structure will not help. It already has structure.
Neither
level 1		 Nothing to follow and nothing to want. Content is rebuilt from the purpose up, not repaired. This is rare, and it is worth saying plainly when you see it.

What follows is the three things that make up the content half.

Purpose: the standard of relevance

Every speech has two purposes, and both are officially defined.

The general purpose is the broad intent: to inform, to persuade, to entertain, or to inspire. “Every speech has a general purpose whether it is defined by the speaker or not.” What it adds is a contract. It tells the audience what they are there to do — learn, decide, enjoy, be moved — and they calibrate how they listen accordingly. Break the contract and a flawless speech is still the wrong speech: a sales pitch delivered to an audience expecting information meets resistance, and a lecture delivered to an audience expecting to be moved switches them off.

The specific purpose is the sharper instrument:

A specific purpose can be expressed as a one-sentence statement about what you hope to accomplish in your speech. The statement should be precisely worded so that anyone who reads your speech will have no doubt about your purpose… It also needs to be worded from the audience's point of view. Clarify exactly what you want the audience to be able to do when you've finished your presentation.

Toastmasters International, Writing a Speech With Purpose (Item 8103), pp. 12–133

What it adds is a standard. With a specific purpose, every sentence can be tested against a question: does this serve the purpose, or does it merely entertain me? Without one, nothing is off-topic, and therefore nothing can be cut — which is why speeches with no purpose are almost always overfull. The specific purpose is also the only way an ending can land. Fundamentals is explicit that the conclusion “is the climax; the destination you were reaching for when you started.” You cannot arrive somewhere you were never going.

Here is the failure this entire section exists to catch, in 8103's own words:

The interesting thing about developing a speech is that you can have a very clear idea of what you want to say without ever considering why you are saying it.

Toastmasters International, Writing a Speech With Purpose (Item 8103), p. 123

That is a content failure described from the inside. The speaker has lived with the material for three weeks and knows it intimately; the audience hears it once, cold. The speaker's certainty about the facts conceals the absence of a point for the listener. You are the only instrument in the room that can detect it — and you detect it the same way any listener does: by noticing that you cannot say what the speech was for.

The test you can run from your seat. In the minute after the speech, before you look at your notes, finish this sentence: “The speaker wanted me to…” If you can complete it, the purpose was delivered. If you cannot, you have found the largest available finding in the speech, and it outranks every mechanical detail you noticed.

Structure: the substitute for a rewind button

The official shape is simple, and every good speech has it whether or not the speaker ever thought about it: “An effective speech is logically organized into an opening, a body, and a conclusion.” The opening “must spark interest in your topic and lead into the subject.” The body “contains the factual support for your purpose.” The conclusion is the climax, and “an inconclusive or apologetic closing can undermine even the best speech.” Between and inside those parts, transitions “act like bridges, connecting your thoughts and ideas and supporting audience members in understanding your message” — needed at five specific points, from the introduction into the body, between main points, and from the final support material into the close.

Now the mechanism, which the rules do not state. A listener cannot rewind. Speech arrives in time, one sentence at a time, and working memory holds only a handful of items at once. Six minutes at a normal speaking rate is roughly 720–960 words. Structure is what turns those hundreds of words into one idea: the point held in one hand, a few markers held in the other. Remove it and the audience keeps whatever was most vivid — usually the stories — and loses the argument. Toastmasters states the effect plainly: “When your speech is well-structured and easy to follow, it is more effective,” and structure is what lets a speech's design “enhance audience members' understanding of your topic and your purpose.”

What structure is not

Structure is not the outline. It is what the audience can hear: signposts, transitions, a return to the opening, a close that closes. A perfect outline that never reaches the audience's ear is not structure; it is a private plan. That is why “I couldn't follow it” is a legitimate finding even when the speech made sense on paper — and why “but it was organised!” is not an answer. Your evidence is what reached you, and the speaker needs to know what did not.

The failure signature is: great parts, no whole. You can retell the anecdote but not the argument; you remember the last point and not the first two; or — the giveaway — you supply your own structure and get it wrong: “Oh, I thought you were arguing X.” One further thing worth noticing: proportion is structural. Four minutes on the first point and forty seconds on the other two tells the audience, accurately, what the speaker believed mattered. If the time budget contradicts the purpose, that is a structure finding, and a precise one.

Interest: whether it deserved the time

“Interesting” is the axis people confuse with entertainment. It is not the same thing. A speech is interesting when the audience has a reason to give it attention: stakes (“what changes for me if I know this?”), relevance (“why is this about me?”), specificity (“when you described the fudge cake, my mouth watered”, not “you described dessert”), surprise, or story. It is well-constructed when the other two parts of this section are in place — a purpose the listener can name, and a structure that carries it there.

Why it is essential: Interest is the only criterion on the form that asks whether the speech should have been given at all. It is also the one a technically accomplished speech can miss entirely. A speaker can be clear, varied, grounded and warm, and have nothing to say — and the form's level 3 gives exactly that combination a name: “engages audience with interesting, well-constructed content.” Both halves are required. Neither one is delivery.

Why you will miss content faults

1. Content is invisible in real time. There is no moment at which “no structure” happens. Purpose and structure are properties of the whole, and the whole does not exist until the speech is over. Delivery gets recorded; content has to be reconstructed. An evaluator who never reconstructs has no content findings — not because the speech had none, but because they never looked.

2. Content faults arrive disguised as delivery faults. Boredom becomes “your voice was flat”. Confusion becomes “you went too fast”. Disbelief becomes “you needed more energy”. Notice that each disguise names a delivery tool, so the speaker will go home and practise that tool. That is the true cost of a symptom-level report: two weeks of work on the wrong thing, with your authority behind it.

3. The form itself points you at delivery. Six of the seven core criteria describe the performance, and the performance is precisely the part you can watch happening. The official warning from Section 1 is aimed at this bias — eye contact and gestures “should not be given so much emphasis that they detract from the basic purpose of the evaluation” — and it is the reason this lesson spends two sections on the half that has one criterion.

The reconstruction test

Sixty seconds after the speech ends, before you look at your notes, answer three questions:

1. Purpose. Can you finish “The speaker wanted me to…”?
2. Structure. Can you name the main points, in the order they came?
3. Value. Can you name one thing you will still remember tomorrow — and say why it earned that?

If 1 or 2 fails, your biggest finding is a content finding. Write it down first, because it is the one you are least likely to recall when you stand up. The test is not a judgement of quality; it is a test of whether the speech survived contact with the only thing that matters — a listener's memory. The reconstruction test is this course's term, not Toastmasters'. The rules under it are theirs: a specific purpose must be stated from the audience's point of view, and the speech must be built to deliver it.

Section 5Choosing your two or three points

The official instruction is unambiguous about the size of an evaluation:

You won't have time to cover everything. Instead, simply select two or three points which you feel are most important and elaborate on them.

Toastmasters International, Effective Evaluation (Item 202), pp. 4–51

Which raises the question the guide does not answer: most important by what measure? Not by how much they bothered you, and not by how easy they are to see. The map gives you a measure, and it has a direction. This course calls it the order of repair: when several things are wrong, fix them from the foundation upward, because a fault in one layer surfaces as symptoms in the layer above it — and repairing the symptom does not repair the cause.

1
Purpose — could I tell what the speech was for?

First because a speech with no purpose has no standard against which anything else can be judged — including your own comments. If you do not know what the speaker was trying to do, you cannot say whether they did it.

2
Structure — could I hold it?

A purpose the audience cannot follow in real time was not delivered, however clearly it existed in the speaker's head. Structure is the delivery mechanism for meaning.

3
Interest — was there anything in it for them?

The layer that decides whether the speech deserved the time at all. It cannot be recovered by any amount of polish, because polish adds no reason to care.

4
Delivery — did it reach me?

Genuine, reportable problems, and still downstream of what the speech was and whether it was built. This is where voice and body live.

5
Polish — filler words, one repeated gesture, a nervous habit

The last few per cent of effect, and historically the first thing a beginner spends all three points on.

Two consequences follow, and they are the practical payoff of this whole lesson.

Upstream fixes dissolve downstream symptoms. A speaker who is certain what the speech is for, and knows the route, tends to slow down, vary more, stand stiller and look up — without practising any of those things. Uncertainty leaks: it appears as hedging, rushing, flatness and apologising. Report the cause and several of the symptoms leave with it. Report only the symptoms and the speaker practises them one at a time, indefinitely.

Downstream fixes cannot repair upstream faults. A fortnight of pace drills produces a smoother speech with no point. This is why “your gestures were lovely and your voice was clear” can be entirely true, entirely accurate, and useless — and why an evaluation that lists six mechanical faults and never says whether the speech achieved anything has failed, however correct each fault was.

The one exception. This is not “content always wins”. If the audience could not hear the speaker, or could not follow the words at all, that outranks the content — because nothing else reached you, and any content judgement you make may be an artefact of not having received the speech. When the pipe is broken, report the pipe first.

The rule that keeps delivery in its place

Report a delivery fault when it changed what you received — not merely when you noticed it. “Your hands were shaking” earns one of your three points when it cost you the opening line. If you noticed it and it cost you nothing, it belongs on the written form, not in your three points. This is Lesson 01's SO WHAT coordinate, turned on your own attention: a behaviour that had no effect on you is an observation, not a finding.

One last thing: the slots are not only for problems. A strength you name precisely is a behaviour the speaker can repeat on purpose, and per Section 3 the delivery strengths are the easy ones to spot. That makes the content strengths the ones worth hunting for — the moment the argument actually turned, the sentence that made the purpose visible. When a speech lands, name the landing and say why it worked. That is the reconstruction test pointed at the other side of the ledger.

Where this goes next

Lessons 03–05 take one part of the anatomy at a time: purpose and structure, then voice, then body. Lesson 06 is about using the 1–5 levels honestly. Everything from here assumes this lesson's map — the criteria, the two families, and the direction of repair. Learn the map before you learn the depths.

DrillsSolidify it

Reading this section produces fluency — the feeling that you understand it. The drills are what produce storage strength: the ability to run the map in a real meeting, on a speech you have never heard, with your heart going. Do them in order, and do not skip the recall.

  4. The order of repair, first to last, and why content comes before delivery.

The effort of failing to recall is the mechanism, not a sign that you learned nothing. Sit with a blank for thirty seconds before you scroll.

Drill 3 · Production Name it and prove it

Recognising a family is not the skill. Diagnosing is. Below are five sentences an evaluator might actually say — the kind that get nods in the room and help nobody. For each one, write down:

  1. Which family, and which criterion.
  2. What you would have had to observe to say it.
  3. The sentence you would actually say out loud, with the behaviour, the location, and the effect on you.

Then reveal the model answers. Note that two of the five have more than one possible diagnosis — that is the point. The criterion name is what forces you to choose.

Symptom 1

“It was a bit flat.”

Model answer

Delivery — vocal variety, and possibly content — interest. “Flat” is a feeling, not a finding. It has at least two distinct causes and the sentence does not say which one you mean.

What you would need to have observed: where in the speech the flatness started, what changed there, and whether the cause was the voice, the material, or both.

If the cause was the voice: “From your second point to the end, every sentence arrived at the same pitch and the same speed, so I stopped hearing where one idea finished and the next began. I noticed it first in the story about your first job.”

If the cause was the material: “The three facts you gave all carried the same weight, so I had no way to know which one mattered most. Losing two of them, and spending the time on the one you cared about, would have given me something to feel.”

WHAT uniform pitch and pace — or undifferentiated material · WHEN the second point onward, first noticed in the story · SO WHAT “I stopped hearing where one idea ended”

Symptom 2

“I got lost in the middle.”

Model answer

Content — structure. “Lost” is almost always about the route, not the voice. The specific fault is usually a missing transition at one of the five points where Toastmasters says a bridge is needed.

What you would need to have observed: the exact place you lost the thread, and what you were still holding when it happened.

Say: “I was with you through the first point. Then you moved into the second without signalling the move, so I was still carrying the first one and I missed the opening of the second. A single sentence — ‘that's the why; here's the how’ — would have carried me across.”

WHAT a missing transition between point one and point two · WHEN the move into the second point · SO WHAT “I was still carrying the first one and missed the opening of the second”

Symptom 3

“You seemed nervous.”

Model answer

Delivery — comfort level. “Seemed nervous” is an inference about the inside of the speaker's head, which you cannot observe and they cannot act on. The criterion is appears comfortable, so report what produced the appearance.

What you would need to have observed: the behaviours, and when they stopped or started.

Say: “For the first minute your weight was shifting from foot to foot and you had both hands on the lectern. It stopped when you got into the story — and that is when I started hearing your words instead of watching your hands.”

WHAT weight shifting, both hands on the lectern · WHEN the first minute, ending when the story began · SO WHAT “I started hearing your words instead of watching your hands”

Symptom 4

“The ending didn't land.”

Model answer

Content — purpose. “Land” is the feeling of arrival, and arrival is only possible if there was a destination. The conclusion is the climax; a close that does not resolve the purpose cannot land, no matter how confidently it is delivered.

What you would need to have observed: what you were still waiting for when the speaker stopped.

Say: “I was still waiting for the idea to resolve when you said thank you. I could tell you what had happened in the story, but not what you wanted me to take away from it. A last line that answered the question your opening raised would have closed the circle for me.”

WHAT an unresolved close · WHEN the final line, at “thank you” · SO WHAT “I was still waiting for the idea to resolve”

Symptom 5

“It felt rushed.”

Model answer

Two candidates, and they need opposite fixes. Delivery — pace: the words themselves came too fast, and the fix is a voice habit. Content — structure: the amount of material forced the speed, and the fix is an editing pass. Getting this wrong sends the speaker to a pace drill when they needed to cut a point.

What you would need to have observed: whether the sentences were genuinely fast, or whether the number of ideas left no room for any of them.

If the pace was the problem: “In the middle section your sentences came out at roughly twice the speed of your opening, and I had to let go of the detail to keep hold of the story.”

If the material was the problem: “You had four points to make in six minutes, so each one got about ninety seconds. I never finished absorbing one before the next arrived. Two of them in full would have been stronger than four in summary.”

WHAT pace, or material volume · WHEN the middle section · SO WHAT “I had to let go of the detail” / “I never finished absorbing one before the next arrived”

Score each of your five diagnoses

  • Did you name a family, not just a feeling? “Delivery” alone is not enough — name the criterion. The criterion is what tells the speaker which tool to practise.
  • Did you say what you would need to have observed? If the diagnosis does not require any particular evidence, it is a mood, not a finding.
  • For the two multi-cause symptoms (1 and 5), did you notice both possibilities? If you chose one without saying why, you may have sent the speaker to the wrong practice.
  • Does your spoken sentence contain WHAT, WHEN and SO WHAT? A diagnosis without an anchor is a verdict with better vocabulary.
  • Did you stay out of the speaker's head? “You seemed nervous” describes a person; “your hands were on the lectern” describes something they can change.

Two or more checks failed on any item? Rewrite that item now. The repetition is the point.

Drill 4 · Field work Count your own attention

This is where the lesson leaves the page. At your next club meeting — or on any speech you can watch end to end — do not try to evaluate. Just watch, and every time you catch yourself forming an observation, press the button for the family it belongs to.

Do not deliberate. If you are unsure, press whichever one it feels like. The point is not to be right; the point is to find out where your attention goes when nobody is steering it.

Almost everyone returns a delivery-heavy count

Delivery is visible, it happens continuously, and it is what your eye is trained to follow. Content is invisible in real time and only checkable by reconstruction. So a beginner's attention lands on delivery by default — usually by a wide margin. That is not a personal failing; it is the bias the official guide warns about, and the whole reason Section 4 exists.

Two questions to ask of your own count

  • Could you finish “The speaker wanted me to…”? If not, you produced a delivery-only evaluation of a speech whose content you never checked — the exact failure this lesson is about. The purpose sentence you just wrote is the finding you would otherwise have missed.
  • Could you name the main points, in order? If you can, structure was working. If you cannot, and you still rated the speech well, ask what standard you were using.

The corrective exercise

Run the same speech again if you can — a recording, or the next speaker at the next meeting — and force yourself to spend the first two minutes on content only: purpose, structure, and whether you cared. Then check whether your delivery observations were really delivery, or whether several of them were content faults wearing a delivery costume.

Bring the ratio to our next session. A count that is still 90/10 after five speeches tells us something worth working on; a count that has moved toward the middle tells us the map has taken hold.
