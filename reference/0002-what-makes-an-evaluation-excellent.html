Reference 02 · The scoring ladders

The Excellence Ladder

Good, great and excellent — defined in Toastmasters' own words, for both the speech and the evaluation of it.

Sources: Items 8101E, 8103E, 8104E, 1179 Use: keep open while evaluating

“Good, great, excellent” is normally a feeling. Toastmasters turns it into something you can point at. There are two ladders in this document and they are easy to confuse: the speech ladder (how good was the speech?) and the evaluation ladder (how good was the evaluation?). Excellent evaluators keep them separate. A mediocre speech and an excellent evaluation are entirely compatible.

Part 1The speech ladder: the five levels

Almost every Pathways evaluation form scores a set of criteria on a 1–5 scale with these names.Ice Breaker Read them once as a set before reading them individually, because the pattern matters more than the individual words.

5
Exemplary

The speaker is not just competent — they are a model. Nothing here needs fixing, and the audience would benefit from watching this again.

4
Excels

Actively strong. The skill is being used as a tool rather than merely present. There is something to refine, but the speaker is ahead of the room.

3
Accomplished

Competent and effective. This is the level where the skill is present, deliberate and working. Level 3 is the definition of “good” — and it is a genuinely good outcome, not a consolation prize.

2
Emerging

The skill is visible in flashes but not under control. Needs “further practice” — the language the rubric repeatedly uses at this level.

1
Developing

Absent, or present in a way that actively harms the speech. Note the rubric's careful wording at level 1 — it is often “destructive” rather than “missing”: distracting gestures, highly distracting movement, little or no attempt to engage.

The pattern that unlocks the whole scale

Levels 1 and 2 are about absence or harm. Level 3 is about presence. Levels 4 and 5 are about deliberate use.

Look at Vocal Variety: level 3 is “uses tone, speed, and volume as tools”; level 4 is “excels at using… as tools”; level 5 is “uses… to perfection.” The difference between a 3 and a 4 is not effort or polish — it is whether the speaker is choosing the variation to serve the meaning, or simply varying because they were told to. That distinction is invisible to an evaluator who is not watching for intent.

Part 2The base criteria, level by level

These seven criteria appear on the great majority of speech evaluation forms. The first seven are the base set; the two movement criteria are added for delivery-focused projects.Ice Breaker · Vocal Variety and Body Language

Clarity

5

Is an exemplary public speaker who is always understood.

4

Excels at communicating using the spoken word.

3

Spoken language is clear and is easily understood.

2

Spoken language is somewhat unclear or challenging to understand.

1

Spoken language is unclear or not easily understood.

What this costs when it fails: clarity is the only criterion where a low score makes every other criterion irrelevant. If the audience cannot understand the words, vocal variety and gestures cannot rescue the speech.

Vocal Variety

5

4

3

Uses tone, speed, and volume as tools.

2

1

Ineffective use of tone, speed, and volume.

Eye Contact

5

Uses eye contact to convey emotion and elicit response.

4

Uses eye contact to gauge audience reaction and response.

3

Effectively uses eye contact to engage audience.

2

Eye contact with audience needs improvement.

1

Makes little or no eye contact with audience.

Notice the direction of travel. At level 3 eye contact is output — engaging the audience. At levels 4 and 5 it becomes input — the speaker is reading the room and responding. That is a fundamentally more advanced skill and it is easy to miss if you only check whether they looked up.

Gestures

5

Fully integrates physical gestures with content to deliver an exemplary speech.

4

Uses physical gestures as a tool to enhance speech.

3

Uses physical gestures effectively.

2

Uses somewhat distracting or limited gestures.

1

Uses very distracting gestures or no gestures.

Note who shares a level. At level 2, “distracting” and “limited” sit side by side — as do “very distracting” and “no gestures at all” at level 1. Nine out of ten beginners assume stillness is safe. The rubric disagrees: no gestures scores the same as very distracting gestures.

Audience Awareness

5

Engages audience completely and anticipates audience needs.

4

Is fully aware of audience engagement/needs and responds effectively.

3

Demonstrates awareness of audience engagement and needs.

2

Audience engagement or awareness of audience requires further practice.

1

Makes little or no attempt to engage audience or meet audience needs.

Comfort Level

5

Appears completely self-assured with the audience.

4

Appears fully at ease with the audience.

3

Appears comfortable with the audience.

2

Appears uncomfortable with the audience.

1

Appears highly uncomfortable with the audience.

Score the appearance, not the person. The rubric says “appears” four times in five levels. A speaker may be terrified and appear comfortable; that is a 3 or better, because what the audience receives is what is being scored.

Interest

5

Fully engages audience with exemplary, well-constructed content.

4

Engages audience with highly compelling, well-constructed content.

3

Engages audience with interesting, well-constructed content.

2

Content is interesting but not well-constructed, or is well-constructed but not interesting.

1

Content is neither interesting nor well-constructed.

Level 2 is the most diagnostic level on the whole scale. It deliberately splits “interesting” from “well-constructed” — telling you that these are two independent axes. A fascinating story told in a muddle is a 2. A tidy, well-organised speech about nothing is also a 2. Being able to name which 2 you are looking at is one of the fastest ways to sound like an experienced evaluator.

The content criteria

Projects focused on speechwriting add purpose-based criteria. These are the hardest to score well because they require you to have understood what the speaker was trying to do.Writing a Speech With Purpose

LevelSpecific PurposeGeneral Speech Purpose
5 Exemplary speech content and organization clearly fulfilled the specific purpose of the speech with precision. Expressed general purpose was met with excellent content and exemplary delivery.
4 The specific purpose was well-executed and the speech style and organization enhanced the speech and topic. Expressed general purpose was well met with excellent content.
3 The specific purpose was clear and supported by the speech style and organization. The speech effectively met the expressed general purpose.
2 The specific purpose was unclear and/or the speech style and organization did not support the specific purpose. The speech came close to meeting the expressed general purpose, but some points or parts missed meeting that goal.
1 The specific purpose was not expressed and the speech style and organization detracted from the speech topic. The speech did not meet the expressed general purpose.
Do not let the mechanics swallow the point

A warning from the official guide, and one of the most commonly violated rules in real clubs:

“Good eye contact, meaningful, natural gestures, and correct grammar contribute to the overall effect of a speech but should not be given so much emphasis that they detract from the basic purpose of the evaluation.”Effective Evaluation

An evaluation that lists six mechanical faults and never mentions whether the speech achieved anything has missed the point — no matter how accurate each fault was. This is the difference between an evaluator and a building inspector.

Part 3The evaluation ladder: the contest ballot

Here is the answer to “what makes an evaluation excellent?” — and it comes from a different document than everything above. The most important observation about this table

The top band is a single point in every row — 40, 30, 15, 15. There is no range for “Excellent.” A 39 out of 40 on Analytical Quality is not Excellent. A 14 out of 15 on Technique is not Excellent.

Whatever the exact arithmetic of the contest, the message is deliberate: excellent means there is nothing left to fix. “Very Good” is the band for a genuinely strong, genuinely flawed evaluation. Most good evaluators live permanently in Very Good, and that is the correct place for them to be. Excellent is a standard, not a destination you settle into.

What each criterion actually demands

The judge's guide defines all four. These definitions are worth knowing verbatim, because they are the closest thing to a specification for this skill.Evaluation Contest Judge's Guide and Ballot

Analytical Quality refers to the effectiveness of the evaluation. Every evaluation should carefully analyze the strengths and weaknesses of the speaker's presentation. Were the evaluator's comments clear and logical? Did the evaluator identify specific strengths and weaknesses of the presentation?

Recommendations are an important part of an evaluation. An evaluator not only points out the strengths and weaknesses of a speech, he/she also offers specific recommendations for improvement. Recommendations should be practical, helpful and positive, and they should enable the speaker to improve his or her next presentation.

Technique refers to the manner in which the evaluator presents his/her comments and recommendations. An evaluator should be sensitive to the feelings and needs of the speaker, yet inspire and encourage the speaker in his/her future speaking efforts.

Summation is how the evaluator concludes the evaluation. The conclusion should briefly summarize the evaluator's comments and suggestions, and be positive and encouraging.

Toastmasters International, Evaluation Contest Judge's Guide and Ballot, p. 2

Three things stand out about the weighting.

Analysis and recommendations are 70% of the score. Technique and summation together are 30%. The criteria that feel most intimidating to a beginner — tone of voice, sensitivity, the closing — are collectively less than half as important as whether you actually saw something true and said what to do about it. If you are nervous, this should be reassuring: the substance carries the weight.

Technique leads with “Sympathetic, Sensitive, Motivational.” Not “confident,” not “articulate,” not “polished.” The criterion is about the effect of your manner on the speaker, which connects directly back to the rule from Lecture 01 — the speaker must walk away eager to work on their next project.

Summation is a scored criterion at all. It is only 15 points, but it exists as a separate line, which tells you the close is not an afterthought. An evaluation that trails off with “…yeah, so, that's what I thought” loses points even if everything before it was excellent.

Part 4The three tiers, side by side

Pulling the two ladders together, here is the practical answer to the question this course was built around. Read it as a progression of what the evaluator can see — because that, more than anything else, is what separates the tiers.

TierWhat it sounds likeWhat it requires of you
Good
Level 3 in your own skill		 “I had trouble hearing you in the last third. I was impressed with the way you used your father's letter. Next time, trying coming out from behind the lectern for the final section — I think it would land harder.” You can observe honestly, name things specifically, avoid advice-as-instruction, and finish on something real. This is a complete, useful evaluation and it is where every competent evaluator operates.
Great
“Very Good” band		 All of the above, plus you identify a pattern rather than a list: “I noticed something that happened three times — whenever you reached a moment of real feeling, your pace doubled. The letter, your mother's illness, the closing. Every time the emotion went up, the tempo went up with it, and I lost the words just when you most wanted me to hear them.” You can hold the whole speech in your head, find a single cause behind several symptoms, connect it to the stated purpose of the project, and offer a recommendation that would fix all three instances at once.
Excellent
The single-point bands		 Everything above, delivered as a well-crafted two-minute speech with its own structure, its own vocal variety and its own memorable close — and with nothing left over. Every observation is precise, every recommendation is actionable, and the speaker leaves wanting to try again immediately. Mastery of all four contest criteria simultaneously, with no residue. In practice this means you also need to be a good speaker, because the evaluation is itself a performance.
The single sentence to remember

A good evaluation reports what happened. A great evaluation explains why it happened. An excellent evaluation does both, in a way that makes the speaker desperate to try again.

Part 5How to score honestly

Three practical problems arise the first few times you fill in a form. They are worth pre-empting.

The cluster-at-4 problem

Most beginners give 4s to everything. This feels generous and safe, and it is neither. A score you did not think about carries no information — the speaker cannot distinguish what you genuinely admired from what you did not notice. The scale is only useful if you use its width. If a speech was genuinely fine and nothing more, 3 is the honest and useful score, and “Accomplished” is not an insult.

The mechanics-over-meaning problem

Covered above, and repeated here because it is the most common failure in real clubs: “should not be given so much emphasis that they detract from the basic purpose of the evaluation.”Effective Evaluation Before you write down a mechanic, ask whether it changed the speech's ability to do its job. If it did not, it is a note, not a finding.

The two-or-three problem

You will notice more than three things. You should still only say two or three. The guide is explicit that “too much information adds stress and may dissuade the speaker from trying again.”Evaluation and Feedback This is not a limit on your observation — it is a limit on your transmission. Write everything on the form; speak about the two or three things that matter most.

What the numbers are actually for

They are not a verdict on the person. They exist for two reasons. First, they force you to commit to a judgement rather than hiding behind vague praise — you cannot write a 5 without deciding the criterion was flawless. Second, accumulated across months, they are how the speaker sees their own progress. This is why the official guidance tells speakers to keep completed evaluations in a single file and revisit them.Evaluation and Feedback Your scores are a longitudinal record. Score them as if someone will read them in a year — because someone will.
