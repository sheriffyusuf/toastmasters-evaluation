You Are Not a Judge

What an evaluation actually is, why most feedback fails, and the two habits that fix it.

Mission: evaluate speeches at Toastmasters Time: ~25 min reading + ~40 min drills Prerequisite: none

The moment you are handed the evaluation form, a quiet pressure arrives. Someone has spent two weeks on this speech, and they have just handed you a pen and said tell me the truth. Almost every new evaluator responds to that pressure by trying to sound like an expert. That instinct is the single largest source of bad evaluations.

What follows is not a set of etiquette rules. It is a set of mechanisms, drawn from Toastmasters' own guidance and from the research on why feedback works and — more often than anyone expects — why it doesn't. Once you see the mechanisms, the rules stop being rules.

Section 1What the role actually is

Toastmasters is unusually blunt about this. The organisation's own guide to evaluating opens by drawing a hard line around what you are and are not:

Your purpose as an evaluator is to provide honest reaction in a constructive manner to the person's efforts… You are not a judge or an authority on speaking or leadership. When you evaluate, you are simply giving your own reaction to the person's speaking or leadership efforts. An evaluation is an opinion, nothing more.

Toastmasters International, Effective Evaluation (Item 202), p. 31

Read that again, because it is doing more work than it first appears. Three separate consequences fall out of it.

1. You are not trying to be right. You are trying to be useful.

There is no correct evaluation to get wrong. There is only a reaction that helps the speaker see something they could not see, and a reaction that does not. This is liberating: it means you never have to perform expertise. You only have to be accurate about what happened inside you while they spoke.

And you are uniquely positioned to report that. A speaker has never watched themselves speak. They have felt themselves speak, which is a completely different sensation — the rush of adrenaline, the paragraph they nearly forgot, the moment the room went quiet. What they cannot access is what the room saw. Their hands. Their pace. The six seconds where their eyes went to the ceiling. You can. That is the entire value you bring.

2. You cannot change them, and you are not responsible for trying.

The guide is explicit: you "cannot change the person's behavior or force the person to accept your ideas and suggested improvements."1 Nor can you demand they repeat a project. The decision to accept your suggestions belongs to the speaker alone.

New evaluators find this frustrating. Experienced ones find it clarifying — it removes the burden of being responsible for someone else's improvement, and replaces it with responsibility for the quality of your observation.

3. You are not speaking for the room.

Your evaluation is one opinion. The guide warns that "your opinions may differ from those of the rest of the audience."1 This has an immediate, concrete linguistic consequence we come to in Section 3.

The two ways an evaluation fails

An evaluation has exactly two failure modes, and they are opposite:

“A harsh evaluation may cause a member to leave the club. An overly kind evaluation may not help the member to improve, making the member frustrated and unhappy.”1

Notice what this means. Being nice is also a failure. An evaluation that flatters is not a safe default — it is a different way of wasting the speaker's fortnight. The goal is not kindness. The goal is honest reaction, constructively delivered. Kindness is how you deliver it, not what you deliver.

Why any of this matters: the uncomfortable finding

You might reasonably assume that feedback, being obviously good for people, mostly helps them. The largest meta-analysis of the question says otherwise, and it is the reason this course takes feedback technique seriously rather than treating it as manners.

Kluger and DeNisi pooled 131 studies — 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations — and found that feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = .41), but that over one-third of them decreased performance.5 A later replication across 435 studies and more than 61,000 participants found a similar average (d = 0.48) alongside enormous variation — enough that the authors concluded feedback "cannot be understood as one construct."7

Read that carefully. It is not that feedback is unreliable. It is that the kind of feedback determines the sign of its effect. Some feedback improves performance. Some feedback makes it worse. Both are common. Which one you produce is a skill, and it is learnable — which is what the rest of this lesson is about.

Section 2The three ingredients every evaluation needs

Strip away style, length and experience, and every useful evaluation contains three things. Even a thirty-second evaluation of a table topic needs all three.

1
The effect it had on you

Not what they did — what happened in you as a result. “I followed every word.” “I stopped listening at the truck story.” This is the most valuable thing you can report, because it is pure information the speaker has no access to.

2
What they did well

Specific and observed. Not “great speech” but “when you described the fudge cake, my mouth watered.”1 The speaker needs to know which behaviours to keep. Most evaluators under-invest here and it is a serious loss — a strength you do not name is a strength they might drop.

3
Where they could improve — plus a specific recommendation

This is two things, and the second is the one people skip. An observation without a suggestion is a complaint. “When you were talking about the truck, I wasn't sure if you were referring to the new one or the old one” is an observation. “Giving each truck a nickname and using it throughout would have made the references clearer to me — and maybe added humour” is a recommendation.1

Why ingredient 3 must be two things

A complaint tells the speaker that something is broken and leaves them to guess at the repair. That is the opposite of help — it hands them a problem and removes the information they need to solve it. Notice also that the model recommendation above is phrased as a possibility with a stated effect: “may have worked better… would have made the references clearer to me.” It is offered, not imposed. That phrasing is not politeness for its own sake — it preserves the speaker's ownership of the decision, which is exactly what makes them act on it.

Section 3First habit: give feedback, not advice

This is the highest-leverage distinction in the discipline, and it is stated with unusual precision in the Pathways guide:

Feedback is information, while advice is an opinion. The evaluator gives the speaker information about what they observed. This helps the speaker to learn how they are being perceived.

Toastmasters International, Evaluation and Feedback (Item 8100), p. 92

The official guide pairs them side by side, and the difference is smaller and more consequential than most people expect:

Advice — an opinionFeedback — information
Don't be so scared.I noticed that you were shaking.
You need to stand up straight.I observed that your upper body was tilted forward.
You have to speak up.I had trouble hearing you.
You need to improve your eye contact.I saw that you were averting your eyes from the audience.
You didn't move enough.I noticed you remained still and in one place when you spoke.

Look at the left column honestly. Every line is probably true. Every line is also something the speaker already suspects, cannot verify, and has no way to act on precisely. Now read the right column the same way. Every line gives them something they did not have.

Four mechanisms — why feedback beats advice

(a) Feedback keeps attention on the task; advice and person-talk drag it to the self

This is the deepest reason, and it is the one that explains everything else. Kluger and DeNisi's theory proposes that feedback takes effect by changing where the listener's attention goes, across three levels:

1
Task-learning processes — attention on the task details

The specific behaviour, at the specific moment. This is where feedback works best.

2
Task-motivation processes — attention on the task in general

“I noticed you were shaking.” About the performance, but not about any particular part of it. Weaker.

3
Meta-task processes — attention on the self

“You were nervous.” “You're a natural.” Attention has left the speech entirely. This is where feedback stops working, and it can reverse.

Their central finding is stated plainly: “FI effectiveness decreases as attention moves up the hierarchy closer to the self and away from the task.”5

Now read the guide's example again with this in mind:

“You were inspiring,” is about the person. “I observed how passionately and energetically you inspired the audience with your personal story,” is about the behaviour.

Toastmasters International, Evaluation and Feedback (Item 8100), p. 122

Toastmasters gives you the rule. Kluger and DeNisi explain why it is a rule: the first sentence sends the speaker's attention to themselves, and a speaker thinking about themselves is not thinking about their speech. That is not a matter of tact. It is a matter of where the limited resource — attention — is being spent.

The test this gives you

Ask of any comment: does this direct attention to the speech, or to the person? If it is the person, you have not just been impolite — you have moved the feedback to the level where it stops working. Rewrite it downward.

(b) Advice smuggles in a diagnosis that may be wrong

“Don't be so scared” presumes three things at once: that the speaker chose to be scared, that fear was the cause of what you saw, and that you know what the alternative looks like for them. Any of those may be false. What if they were cold? What if they had just sprinted up a flight of stairs? What if the trembling was an adrenaline response they cannot switch off and know perfectly well about?

Feedback makes no such claim. “I noticed that you were shaking” is a statement about your observation, and it is very hard to be wrong about your own observation. You are reporting data. Advice is a conclusion drawn from data — and the speaker is better placed than you to draw it, because they know their own history, their own body, and the specific thing they were working on.

(c) Feedback leaves the speaker holding the wheel

This is the mechanism that actually produces change. The guide's instruction to speakers is that applying feedback is their responsibility, and it explicitly protects their right to choose differently from you: “Meeting your personal goals, even if those goals differ from the goals your evaluator laid out for you, is your prerogative.”2

Advice collapses that. It says: here is the fix. The speaker's options become comply or reject, and rejection is cheap when the fix is someone else's. Feedback leaves the gap between observation and remedy deliberately open, and that gap is where the speaker's own decision lives. A decision the speaker made is a decision the speaker will act on.

There is a second-order benefit. The guide asks evaluators to limit suggestions to two or three points, because “too much information adds stress and may dissuade the speaker from trying again.”2 Feedback, being shorter and non-prescriptive, naturally crowds less.

(d) Feedback cannot be argued with

This is the mechanism that will save you as a beginner. Suppose you tell a speaker “you need to speak up,” and it turns out they were perfectly audible to everyone but you. You are now in a dispute you cannot win, and the speaker has a reason to discard your whole evaluation.

Now suppose you said “I had trouble hearing you.” That is unfalsifiably true. It happened. Even the person who heard them perfectly well cannot contradict you. You have made a claim that costs you nothing to be wrong about, and the speaker can still take the useful part — someone in this room had trouble, that is worth knowing.

The self-test for any comment you are about to make

Could the speaker reasonably reply “no I didn't”? If yes, you have written advice or a judgement, and you should rewrite it. “I had trouble hearing you” cannot be answered with “no you didn't.” “You need to speak up” can.

The sentence starters to use

The guide recommends “words that describe your own reactions to the speaker,” and gives the phrasings it wants to hear:1

  • “I was impressed with…”
  • “I was confused about…”
  • “When I heard…”
  • “I think the speech's purpose would have been clearer if…”
  • “I liked it when…”

And the rule that generates all of them: “I” messages keep the focus on behaviours, not the person.2

The words to strike from your vocabulary

The guide supplies an explicit blacklist:1

BannedWhy it fails
“we think”, “we believe”, “the audience would have”, “the audience didn't understand” You are not the audience. These imply you speak for others and immediately invite disagreement from anyone who felt differently.
“they say”, “one must”, “people are” Impersonal phrasing that hides who is actually making the claim — and implies the evaluation is aimed at someone other than the person in front of you.
“good leaders don't”, “that was the wrong thing to say”, “if you want to do it right, you must” Judgement words. They declare a universal standard and put the speaker on trial against it.
“you did”, “you were” Describes the person rather than the behaviour, which is the exact phrasing that pushes attention up to the self level — where feedback stops working.
“never”, “always” Exaggerations that “detract from your message.” One instance is not a pattern, and an inaccurate claim gives the speaker licence to ignore the accurate part.

One more rule, easy to break without noticing: do not repeat a point once you have made it. The guide's reason is precise — repetition “can sound like nagging.”1 Say it once, clearly, then move on.

Section 4Second habit: anchor your feedback in time

Section 3 gives you a filter. Run any comment through it and you can tell whether it is feedback or advice, task-directed or self-directed. That filter is necessary. It is not sufficient, and here is the hole in it.

A comment that passes every test in Section 3 — and is still nearly useless

“I noticed you were shaking.”

It is feedback, not advice. It reports your observation. It cannot be answered with “no I wasn't.” It is task-directed rather than personal. It is, by every rule so far, correct.

And the speaker can do nothing with it. They cannot check it. They cannot tell whether it was one moment or the whole six minutes. They cannot rehearse against it, because nobody, including you, knows when it happened.

What is missing is a location. This section is about supplying one, and about why it is not the finishing touch on good feedback — it is the part that makes the rest of the feedback function at all.

What the research says about specificity

Feedback researchers have a word for this, and a definition worth knowing exactly. Shute's review of the field defines feedback specificity as “the level of information presented in feedback messages.” Specifically — and note the wording — specific or elaborated feedback “provides information about particular responses or behaviors beyond just their accuracy.”6

“Particular responses or behaviors.” Not behaviours in general. Particular ones. Then comes the list of what happens when you fail at this, and it is worth reading slowly, because every item is a thing you have probably felt as a recipient of vague feedback:

Feedback lacking in specificity may cause students to view it as useless and/or frustrating. It can also lead to uncertainty about how to respond to the feedback and may require greater information processing activity on the part of the learner to understand the intended message… Uncertainty and cognitive load can lead to lower levels of learning… or even reduced motivation to respond to the feedback.

Valerie J. Shute, Focus on Formative Feedback (2008), §3.3, summarising Williams (1997), Fedor (1991), Bangert-Drowns et al., Kluger & DeNisi (1996)6

That last clause is the one to sit with. Vague feedback does not merely fail to help. It costs the speaker something: it spends their attention on the puzzle of working out what you meant, and it can reduce their willingness to try again at all. You have introduced cognitive load without introducing information. That is a straightforwardly bad trade.

There is direct experimental support too. Phye and Sanders tested general advice against specific feedback on a problem-solving task and found the specific feedback “clearly superior to general advice” on a retention task.6

The mechanism: why a location keeps attention on the task

Now connect this back to the attention hierarchy from Section 3, because the two findings explain each other.

Watch what happens to a single observation as you add a location:

1
Self level

“You were nervous.” — attention on the person. Feedback stops working here.

2
Task-motivation level

“I noticed you were shaking.” — attention on the performance in general, but on no particular part of it. Better, but the speaker has nowhere to look.

3
Task-learning level

“I noticed your hands were shaking through the opening, and it had settled by the time you reached the grandmother story.” — attention on a specific behaviour at a specific moment. This is the only level at which the speaker can do anything.

That is the mechanism. Adding a location is what moves a comment down the hierarchy, from “about the performance” to “about the task.” Every time you omit when something happened, you are pushing the comment one level up, toward the self, where it stops working.

And notice how Toastmasters' own model example already does this, without ever naming it. The guide's illustration of good feedback is not “the references were unclear.” It is:

“When you were talking about the truck, I wasn't sure if you were referring to the new one or to the old one.”1

The organisation models the practice in every example it gives, and its standing instruction is simply “Be specific.”1 The contest ballot scores “specific strengths and weaknesses” as a defined component of Analytical Quality, worth 40 of 100 points.4

On sourcing — what is Toastmasters' and what is mine

Being straight with you about attribution, since it affects how much weight to put on each claim.

  • Toastmasters': “Be specific.” “Identify specific strengths and weaknesses.” The truck example's phrasing. All directly sourced.
  • Research: that specificity improves retention and that vagueness produces uncertainty, cognitive load and reduced motivation (Shute, summarising the field); that feedback effectiveness falls as attention moves from task to self (Kluger & DeNisi).
  • My synthesis: the three coordinates below, and the word “anchor.” Toastmasters does not use this vocabulary. It is a teaching device I have built to make the research and the official examples operational. If you meet a Toastmaster who disagrees with the framing, they are not disagreeing with Toastmasters.

The anchor test

Ask this before you speak

Could the speaker rewind to that exact moment and watch it?

If yes, you have a finding. If no, you have an impression — and impressions are much weaker currency, though not worthless (see “when you cannot anchor”, below).

The three coordinates of usable feedback

Every piece of feedback that survives the anchor test specifies three things. Learn these as a checklist you run in your head while the speaker is still talking.

1
WHAT — the behaviour

Observable and physical, not a trait. “Your hands were shaking” — not “you were nervous.” “Your pace doubled” — not “you seemed rushed.” If you cannot photograph it or measure it, it is not a behaviour.

2
WHEN — the location

Where in the speech. A section, a story, a sentence, a minute, a transition — any handle the speaker can grab. “Through the opening.” “When you moved to the second story.” “In the final paragraph.” This is the coordinate people omit, and it carries the most weight.

3
SO WHAT — the effect on you

What the behaviour did to you as a listener. This is what makes it an evaluation rather than an inspection report, and it is the coordinate beginners omit second-most-often. “And it settled me.” “And I stopped hearing the words.” “And I wrote that line down.”

Note that WHICH behaviours you report is a separate skill — that is the entire subject of Lessons 03 to 05, where we go criterion by criterion through voice, body language and content. This lesson is about the form of the sentence, whichever behaviour you are reporting.

Why WHEN carries so much weight — four jobs

It would be reasonable to think the location is a detail. It is doing four separate jobs at once.

  1. It holds the feedback at the level where it works. The Kluger and DeNisi mechanism above. No location means the comment floats up the hierarchy toward the self, and weakens.

  2. It makes the feedback verifiable. The classic review by Kulhavy and Stock found that effective feedback supplies two distinct things: verification — the simple judgement of whether something was correct — and elaboration — the cues that guide the learner.6 A location is what lets the speaker verify. Without it they must take your word for it; with it, they can go and look. And a speaker who has verified something for themselves will believe it far more durably than one who has merely been told.

  3. It makes the feedback rehearsable. A speaker preparing their next run-through can only work on what they can locate. “Speak more slowly” cannot be rehearsed; there is no moment to attach it to. “There were three places where the emotion rose and the tempo rose with it — the letter, your mother's illness, and the closing” can be rehearsed, three times, deliberately, until it changes. This is why specific feedback beats general advice on retention: it is the only kind that survives into practice.

  4. It separates a habit from an accident. One instance is a data point. Three instances at three identifiable moments is a pattern — and only a pattern is worth changing. See below.

One-off or pattern: the skill of counting

While you are watching an evaluation speech, you are doing two things at once: noticing behaviours, and counting them. The count is itself information, and it changes what you should say.

What you countedWhat it meansWhat to say
Once Possibly an accident — a slip, a distraction, an off night. Usually say nothing, or mention it very lightly. Reporting a single instance as a fault creates a problem the speaker may not have.
Two or three times Emerging pattern. Worth naming. Name the instances. “I noticed this three times…”
Throughout A default behaviour — usually unconscious. Name it as the headline finding. This is the highest-value thing you can report, because the speaker almost certainly cannot feel it. You cannot feel your own default.

This is where the difference between a good evaluation and a great one starts. A list of three separate observations is good. One observation with three instances pointing at a single cause is great — because it gives the speaker one thing to change instead of three, and it explains itself.

Compare:

Three findings, no patternOne finding, three proofs
“Your pace got fast in the letter. You rushed the illness section. The closing felt hurried.” “I noticed something that happened three times. Every time you reached a moment of real feeling — the letter, your mother's illness, the closing — your pace doubled. Each time the emotional temperature rose, the tempo rose with it, and I lost the words precisely when you most wanted me to hear them.”

The right-hand version is not longer for the sake of it. It delivers one diagnosis, one cause, and therefore one thing to practise. It also tells the speaker something they could never have worked out alone: that the three separate problems were the same problem.

The sentence to remember

A good evaluation reports what happened. A great evaluation explains why it happened. An excellent evaluation does both, in a way that makes the speaker desperate to try again.

Before and after

Unanchored — an impressionAnchored — a finding
“I noticed you were shaking.” “I noticed your hands were shaking through the opening — and it had settled by the time you reached the second point.”
“I had trouble hearing you.” “I lost you twice — once when you turned towards the flipchart, and once in the final paragraph.”
“Your gestures were distracting.” “Your right hand kept going into your pocket — about four times in the first minute — and each time my eyes followed it away from your face.”
“I liked the story.” “I stopped taking notes when you got to the letter. That was the moment the speech became about something.”

Look at how much longer the right-hand column is, and notice that none of the extra words are padding. Each one is either a location or an effect. That is the whole budget of extra length you are allowed — everything else you add is noise.

When you cannot anchor — and you sometimes won't

There will be times when you have a feeling about a speech and cannot point at the moment that caused it. Three honest options, in order of preference.

Option 1 — say so, and downgrade the claim

“I don't have a specific moment for this, so take it for what it's worth — I had a general sense of distance from you in the middle section.”

This is far better than manufacturing a false location. It tells the speaker exactly how much weight to give the comment, and it demonstrates the intellectual honesty you would want from your own evaluator.

Option 2 — say nothing, and watch for it next time

You are not obliged to comment on everything you noticed. An unanchored impression is often better held in reserve: if the same feeling appears in the speaker's next speech, you now have two instances and something to say.

Never invent a location

A false “during your second point” is worse than no anchor at all. The speaker will go looking for something that was not there, fail to find it, and begin to distrust your other observations — including the accurate ones. You would be spending your credibility to buy precision you do not have.

Section 5The shape: Commend → Recommend → Commend

Almost everyone has heard of the “feedback sandwich.” Toastmasters' own version is more precisely named Commend – Recommend – Commend (CRC), and understanding the sequence matters more than the metaphor.3

MoveWhat it does
Commend The speaker relaxes. Their guard comes down and their ability to hear the next sentence goes up.
Recommend Delivered while attention is at its highest, and framed as information rather than verdict.
Commend Establishes what they walk away feeling about the whole experience. This is the part they will remember on the drive home.

The reason this ordering works is not politeness, and Section 3 has now given you the vocabulary for it: a speaker attending to themselves cannot attend to the task. A defensive listener is a listener whose attention has climbed to the meta-task level, which is exactly where feedback stops working. Sequencing is a technical requirement for the information to land — the same way breath support is a technical requirement for projecting your voice.

The honest caveat about the sandwich

The sandwich has a well-documented weakness: delivered mechanically, it becomes recognisable as a formula, and the audience — including the speaker — can see the bread coming. Toastmasters Magazine has published pieces arguing against the “sandwich of affirmation” precisely because ritual replaces substance.4

The fix is not to abandon the shape. It is to make the commendations real. “Great job!” is bread. “I noticed your pace dropped when you got to your father's letter, and the room went still” is a commendation the speaker can actually use as a model — and note that it has all three coordinates.

What actually matters, underneath the shape, is the ratio. The guide's instruction is to “seek a balance that favors positive remarks over negative.”2 CRC is one way of achieving that balance. It is not the only one, and real evaluations are not all 1–1–1.

Why you must finish well

The speaker or leader should always walk away from the meeting feeling motivated and eager to begin working on his next project. How you finish your evaluation often determines whether a speaker is motivated or unmotivated.

Toastmasters International, Effective Evaluation (Item 202), p. 51

This is not sentimentality, and Section 1 gave you the number that proves it: over a third of feedback interventions reduce performance.5 A demoralised speaker practises less; a motivated speaker practises more. An evaluation that is accurate but discouraging fails the same way a flattering one does — it produces no next attempt worth judging.

Section 6Where this sits on the ladder

Everything in this lesson is the floor, not the ceiling. Correct feedback language, an anchor, and a defensible shape make your evaluation useful — what Toastmasters would score as a solid 3, “Accomplished.”

The top of the ladder is defined by a different document entirely: the Evaluation Contest Judge's Guide, which scores evaluations out of 100 across four criteria. Two of those four — Analytical Quality (40 points) and Recommendations (30 points) — are about the substance of what you saw, and you cannot earn them until you know what to look for. That is what the next several lessons build.

The running example “The Last Bus” — a six-minute speech

Drills 3 and 4 both work on the same speech. Read the timeline once; you will need it to supply anchors.

TimeWhat happens
0:00–0:40Opening. The speaker describes standing at a bus stop in the rain at eleven at night, and the specific sound of the timetable flapping in the wind. He shifts his weight from foot to foot while he talks.
0:40–1:20Introduces his grandmother. States that the speech is about “the person who taught me to wait.”

3:10–4:30Story two. His own wait — two years in a relationship that was failing. Delivered at a noticeably faster pace than story one.

5:30–6:00Conclusion. Returns to the bus stop image. His volume drops to almost a whisper in the final paragraph.
Drill 3 · Production Rewrite advice as feedback — and anchor it

You have just watched “The Last Bus.” Below are five things you caught yourself about to say. Each field is separate, so the question stays in front of you while you write.

For each one, write a version that has all three coordinates: WHAT the behaviour was, WHEN it happened, and SO WHAT effect it had on you. Then reveal the model answer and score yourself.

Advice 1

“You need to relax.”

Model answer

“For the first forty seconds you were shifting your weight from foot to foot. It stopped the moment you started the grandmother story, and from then on you looked settled.”

WHAT weight shifting · WHEN the first forty seconds · SO WHAT implied — the contrast with the settled second half is what carries it here.

Advice 2

“You have to speak up.”

Model answer

“I lost you in the final paragraph. Your volume came right down to a whisper — and it was close enough to moving that I was leaning forward to catch it, but I missed the last few words entirely.”

WHAT volume dropped to near-whisper · WHEN the final paragraph · SO WHAT “I missed the last few words entirely”

Note that this is a case where the behaviour itself may have been a choice — the quiet ending is a real technique. That is exactly why the feedback form matters: it lets the speaker decide whether the effect was the one they intended.

Advice 3

“You didn't move enough.”

Model answer

“You stayed anchored in the same spot for the whole six minutes. That worked for me in the two stories — I was with you. In the transition at the three-minute mark, though, I would have liked something physical to mark the shift, because I wasn't immediately sure we were leaving the first story.”

WHAT no change of position · WHEN throughout, with the transition called out specifically · SO WHAT “I wasn't immediately sure we were leaving the first story”

Notice what this rewrite does that the advice could not: it separates the occasions when stillness helped from the one occasion when it cost something. “You didn't move enough” is a blanket verdict. This is a finding about a specific moment.

Advice 4

“Stop saying ‘um’.”

Model answer

“There was a cluster of filler words around the transition — four or five in about twenty seconds, right where you said ‘I didn't understand that for twenty years.’ I'd been completely inside the story until then, and that is the moment I came out of it.”

WHAT cluster of filler words · WHEN the transition, in about twenty seconds · SO WHAT “that is the moment I came out of it”

Counting matters here. Four or five in twenty seconds is a pattern with a location. Saying “you said ‘um’ eleven times” without a location gives the speaker a number and nothing to do with it.

Advice 5

“You should slow down — you were too fast in the middle.”

Model answer — the pattern version

“Something happened three times. Every time you reached a moment of real feeling — the line about her missing the bus on purpose, the two years in the relationship, and the closing — your pace increased. Each time the emotion went up, the tempo went up with it, and I lost the individual words exactly where you most wanted me to hear them.”

WHAT pace increasing under emotion · WHEN three named instances · SO WHAT “I lost the individual words”

This is the strong form. It is one finding with three proofs, it points at a single cause, and it gives the speaker one thing to rehearse instead of three.

Score each of your five rewrites

  • Does it start with “I” or a plain report of observation? If not, you drifted back into describing the person.
  • Could the speaker reply “no I didn't”? If yes, rewrite it. “You didn't relax” can be argued with. “Your weight was shifting” cannot.
  • Does it answer WHEN? Could the speaker rewind to that exact moment? If not, you have an impression and you should either label it as one or drop it.
  • Does it answer SO WHAT? What did the behaviour do to you? If your sentence stops at the behaviour, you have filed an inspection report.
  • Is it at the task level, not the self level? Nothing about the speaker's character, personality, or talent.
  • Did you skip the fix? If you caught yourself writing “so next time you should…”, that is the advice habit returning. The fix belongs in the recommendation, and the recommendation comes after the observation.

Fewer than five of six checks passed on any item? Rewrite that item now. The repetition is the point.

Drill 4 · Anchoring Turn notes into findings

Back to “The Last Bus.” These are raw notes, jotted during the speech. Every one of them is honest — you really did notice these things. Every one is also unusable as written, because none of them has a location.

Rewrite each into a finding with all three coordinates. Use the timeline above.

Raw note 1

“You were hard to hear.”

Model answer

“I lost you in the final paragraph — your volume dropped to almost a whisper on the last two sentences, and I missed them.”

WHAT volume · WHEN the final paragraph, last two sentences · SO WHAT “I missed them”

Raw note 2

“Good use of pausing.”

Model answer

“The pause after ‘She missed the bus on purpose’ held for what felt like three full seconds, and the room went completely still. That silence did more work than the sentence did.”

WHAT a held pause · WHEN after the key line in story one, around 2:35 · SO WHAT “the room went completely still”

Raw note 3

“You seemed nervous.”

Model answer

“Your weight was shifting from one foot to the other for the whole of the opening — the first forty seconds or so. It stopped the moment you got into the grandmother story, and you looked settled from then on.”

WHAT weight shifting (not “nervous” — that was your interpretation, and interpretations belong to the speaker) · WHEN the opening · SO WHAT the contrast: it stopped, which is itself encouraging information

This is the most instructive of the six. Note how much of the original note was your inference rather than your observation. The rewrite contains no diagnosis at all — and it is more useful, because the speaker can act on it.

Raw note 4

“That was a great line about the rain.”

Model answer

“The line about the rain — ‘it rained the way it does when the sky has nothing better to do’ — I wrote that one down. It was about four lines into the opening, and it bought you my attention for the rest of the speech.”

WHAT a specific sentence, quoted · WHEN four lines into the opening · SO WHAT “it bought you my attention for the rest of the speech”

Quoting the line is the anchor. It also does something else valuable: it tells the speaker which register worked, so they can find it again deliberately rather than by luck.

Raw note 5

“You lost me at one point.”

Model answer

“At the transition — when you said ‘I didn't understand that for twenty years’ — I wasn't sure whether we were moving to a new story or still inside the first one. It took me until you mentioned the relationship to work out that we had changed scene.”

WHAT an unclear transition · WHEN at 2:50, the specific line · SO WHAT “it took me until you mentioned the relationship to work out that we had changed scene”

That last line is the recommendation hiding inside the observation. It tells the speaker precisely how long the confusion lasted, which tells them how much signposting is missing.

Raw note 6

“Your hands were distracting.”

Model answer

“Your right hand kept going into your pocket — I counted four times in the first minute. Each time my eyes followed it, and I'd have to find your face again. It stopped completely once you got to the grandmother story.”

WHAT hand going to pocket, counted · WHEN four times in the first minute · SO WHAT “I'd have to find your face again”

Three things are working here that were absent from the raw note: the count, the location, and the fact that it stopped. The last one is genuinely good news and the speaker would never have known it.

The harder question

Notes 1 and 3 both point at the same underlying thing — the speaker's state in the opening. Notes 5 and 6 both stop by about the two-minute mark.

What single finding could you report that covers notes 1, 3, 5 and 6 without becoming four separate items? Remember the guide's instruction to limit yourself to two or three points.

A possible answer: “There is a clear line in this speech at about two-and-a-half minutes. Before it, you were shifting your weight, your hand was going to your pocket, and I was working to follow you. After it — from the grandmother story onwards — all of that stopped and I stopped working. Whatever you do to prepare for the opening, do more of it, because the second half of this speech is the one I'll remember.”

That is one finding, four instances, and a commendation folded into it. It is the shape of a great evaluation rather than a good one.

Drill 5 · Field work Tally a real evaluator

This is where the lesson leaves the page. At your next club meeting — the next one, not “sometime” — listen to one evaluator and count. Every time they say something that is information about what they observed, press Feedback. Every time they say something that is an opinion about what the speaker should do, press Advice.

Do not try to judge whether the evaluation was good. Just count. You will learn two things at once: how quickly your ear develops, and how much advice is in the average evaluation — which is usually more than anyone expects.

0 Feedback
0 Advice

Then run a second tally on the same evaluator. This is the new part, and it is the harder one. Every time they say something that names a location in the speech — a section, a story, a moment, a quoted line — press Anchored. Every time they make a comment that could have applied to any speech, press Floating.

0 Anchored
0 Floating

Both tallies are saved on this device, so you can keep counting across several meetings. Bring the numbers to your next session with me — the ratio between them tells us a lot about what to work on next.

Once you have your counts, answer one question: of the advice you heard, how much do you think the speaker could actually have acted on without asking a follow-up question? That question is the reason this course exists.
