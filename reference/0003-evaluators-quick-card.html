Reference 03 · Field card

Evaluator's Quick Card

Everything you need while the speech is happening and in the two minutes before you stand up.

Read time: 3 minutes Prints: 2 pages

1Before the meeting

1 Read the speaker's project. Every project has different objectives. You cannot evaluate against objectives you have not read, and evaluating the wrong thing is the most common way to waste a speaker's fortnight.
2 Get the evaluation form for that exact project. It tells you what to look for.
3 Talk to the speaker. Two questions: What are you working on? and Is there anything specific you want me to watch? A speech evaluated against the speaker's own goal is worth ten evaluated against yours.
4 Ask what feedback they have already had. Do not repeat an evaluation they have already received — it teaches them nothing and tells them you did not ask.
5 Decide to speak about two or three things only. You will notice more. Say less. “Too much information adds stress and may dissuade the speaker from trying again.”

2While they speak — watch four channels

Write in the margin of the form, not in sentences. Timestamp everything — “lost the thread at 3:10” is actionable; “lost the thread” is not.

ChannelWatch for
Content Did I know the purpose? Was there a structure, and did I follow it? Did the transitions carry me? Did the opening earn my attention? Did the close land, or did it just stop? Did I get anything?
Voice Pitch — varied, or flat?
Tone — did the emotion match the words?
Volume — could I hear, without strain? Did it vary?
Pace — did it change, or stay constant throughout?
Pauses — used deliberately, or just gaps?
Body Posture & stance — grounded or collapsed?
Movement — purposeful, or unintentional (swaying, rocking, weight-shifting)?
Gestures — did they illustrate the meaning, or just happen?
Eye contact — across the whole room, or one spot? Reading the room, or broadcasting at it?
Face — did it agree with the message?
Effect on you The most valuable thing you can report and the thing the speaker cannot know. Where did you lean in? Where did you drift? What do you still remember right now, before writing anything down?
Three things not to do while they speak

Do not write so much that you stop watching — you will end up evaluating your notes. Do not evaluate things outside the speaker's control (the room, the projector, the audience). Do not start composing your evaluation before the speech ends; the ending is usually where the diagnosis lives.

3Two minutes before you stand up

Now, and not before now, choose your two or three points. For each one, fill in both halves:

a
The observation

What you saw or felt, located in time. “I had trouble following the second point — I wasn't sure whether the truck was the new one or the old one.”

b
The recommendation

A practical, positive suggestion for the next presentation. Offered, not imposed. “Giving each truck a nickname and using it throughout might have made it clearer — and could have added humour.”

An observation without a recommendation is a complaint. A recommendation without an observation is an unexplained order. You need both, in that order.

4The shape of what you will say

1
Commend

Something specific and real. Name the behaviour and its effect, not the verdict. Not “great energy” but “your pace picked up the moment you got to the argument with your brother, and I sat forward.”

2
Recommend

Your observation and your suggestion, delivered while attention is highest. Two or three points, no more. Say each one once.

3
Commend

Close on the effect they had on you. This is what they will remember on the drive home. If they improved at something, say so and congratulate them.

The finish line: the speaker should always walk away from the meeting feeling motivated and eager to begin working on their next project. If they do not, your evaluation failed — however accurate it was.

5Phrase patterns

Never saySay instead
You need to speak up.I had trouble hearing you in the last third.
You should stand up straighter.I noticed your upper body was tilted forward.
Don't be so nervous.I noticed your hands were shaking at the start.
You didn't move enough.I noticed you stayed in one place while you spoke.
You need better eye contact.I saw you were looking over the audience's heads.
Your conclusion was weak.I felt the speech stop rather than land.

Openers to reach for

“I noticed…” · “I saw…” · “I felt…” · “I had trouble…” · “I was impressed with…” · “I was confused about…” · “When I heard…” · “I liked it when…” · “I think the purpose would have been clearer if…”

Words struck from your vocabulary

“you need to” · “you should” · “you must” · “you were” · “we think” · “we believe” · “the audience would have” · “the audience didn't understand” · “they say” · “one must” · “good speakers don't” · “that was the wrong thing to do” · “always” · “never”

The one test, if you remember nothing else

Before you say any comment, ask: could the speaker reasonably reply “no I didn't”?

If yes, it is advice or a judgement — rewrite it. “I had trouble hearing you” cannot be answered with “no you didn't.” “You need to speak up” can.

6After you sit down

Return the manual with your written evaluation.
Ask if they have any questions, and check they did not misunderstand anything.
Ask: “Could I have said anything differently that would have been more helpful?” This is how you get better at this, and almost nobody does it.
Stop talking about it. Do not repeat a point once you have made it — it sounds like nagging.
The three failure modes, in one place

Too harsh — the member stops coming.
Too kind — the member stops improving, and gets frustrated.
Too much — the member is overwhelmed and changes nothing.

All three fail the same way: no next attempt worth judging.
